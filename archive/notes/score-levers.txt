SCORE LEVERS
How the pipeline works, where its blind spots are, and the remaining ideas ranked

Written on 1 August 2026, meant to make sense when read cold. Line numbers refer to
notebooks/v7_debug/arc_solver.py unless I say otherwise. Anything marked [measured] was
computed that day from paper/data/pool_dump.json, a 60-output slice run under deployed
conditions, using scripts/pool_census.py and scripts/pool_vote.py.

A note on scope. No outside library came with the request, so this audits the library we
actually deploy, meaning the v7 pipeline source plus every measurement the project has
recorded. I can't verify the premise that we're "1.0 point behind the cutoff" from here.
Our recorded board state is 30.97 with fifth place at 33.89, and the target Ryan set is 32
or more, which is the same band. Nothing below depends on the exact cutoff.


1. WHAT THE PIPELINE ACTUALLY IS

Each task goes through four stages, and each one can lose the answer. Test-time training
builds an adapter, a depth-first search builds a pool of candidates, the candidates get
rescored under augmentations, and the top two are submitted.

I checked these facts against the source that day rather than relying on memory.

1a. Test-time training uses a rank-256 LoRA with alpha 32 and rslora, over 16 augmented
    views (augment(n=16), line 288). The loss only covers the answer, using token ids 11
    and 12 to find it (lines 60 to 76).

1b. The search is turbo_dfs (line 79), a recursive depth-first walk over the 16-token
    vocabulary. Its max_score is a cap on cumulative suffix NLL, set by default to
    -log(0.2) (line 256). So every candidate it emits has a total conditional probability
    above 0.2, which means each view can emit at most four. The pool is small by design,
    not because the time ran out.

1c. The walk runs all views in lockstep, one token per view per forward pass (lines 96 to
    117). Children are sorted best first locally (line 94), but the overall order is depth
    first, so the walk stays inside a subtree until it's used up. Each call has a 540-second
    limit (line 96).

1d. The inner loop talks to the CPU at every node, with logits.float().cpu().log_softmax(-1)
    on line 81 and an .item() call per child on line 87. With only 16 tokens the softmax
    itself is free. The likely cost is the forced sync between GPU and CPU at each node,
    which is exactly what the LB33 perfpatch removes. rig_dfsbench.py is measuring whether
    that matters on our hardware.

1e. The views decode in four batches of four, always in the same order (lines 382 to 391),
    under ARC_TASK_TIMEOUT=1800 (line 403). When a task runs long, the later batches get
    skipped without a word. Views 8 to 15 never decode, and the multiplicity count the
    selector relies on is built from half the views it should have. Nothing in the log
    records how often this happens.

1f. For selection, candidates are rescored under about eight augmentations with duplicates
    removed (known_scores, lines 435 to 455). Then kgmon, which is multiplicity minus the
    mean augmentation NLL, picks the top two.

The v7_debug copy also carries the AIF arm, a penalty based on the number of connected
components (lines 426 to 430). That's v7.5a, which was built but never submitted.


2. CLOSED BY MEASUREMENT. DON'T PROPOSE THESE AGAIN.

- Restarting with new seeds, or ensembling adapters. The union across seeds equals the best
  single seed, and once the same-seed control is subtracted the extra diversity is +0.008.
  This came from a 65-cell probe.
- Synthetic data aimed at the tasks we miss. No structural feature separates missed tasks
  from reached ones (AUC about 0.5 to 0.65), and covering the tail of the best feature does
  worse than picking at random.
- Rescoring the pool with the base model before adaptation. Nothing on the outputs where
  wrong candidates dominate. The domination belongs to the candidates, not the adapter.
- Validating the 2B rescorer on the public evaluation set. The 2B is contaminated on public
  eval, with a median truth NLL of 0.0002, and that became the audit section of the paper.
  Be careful about what this does not close, which is using the 2B on the hidden set. See F3.
- Trusting the score shown beside a public notebook. See the 28.06 control.
- [measured] Voting cell by cell across the candidates. It rebuilds none of the 41 missing
  true answers, whether the votes are uniform, weighted by multiplicity or weighted by
  exp(-augNLL). The candidates' mistakes are correlated through the model's beliefs, so the
  consensus is confidently wrong at exactly the cells that matter.
- [measured] Making the second attempt more different from the first. The two kgmon picks
  are already different, with a median Hamming distance of 12 and only 12 of 56 pairs within
  two cells. There's little to gain.
- [measured] A1, the reweighted monotone selector, is dead by the bar set in advance. Fitted
  on everything it reaches 16 of the 18 the bound allows, but under leave-one-task-out it
  gets 14 against kgmon's 15, a loss of one. The weights (1, 0, 4) won in 37 of 39 folds. The
  three-output gap is real but can't be learned from 60 outputs, and tuning the weights just
  memorises the outputs that leaked. See scripts/pool_selector_fit.py.
- [measured 4 August] A2, the invariant-mining filter, is also dead by its own bar. It
  needed two recovered with no true answers killed. It recovered none, and the full library
  of 15 types killed 11 of the 19 true answers. That's built in, not bad luck. With two or
  three demonstrations, coincidences are common, and ARC-2 test pairs are built to break
  exactly those. In 38007db0, for instance, the training outputs are 19 by 7 and the test
  answers 29 by 8. The safe invariant types are properties visible in the demonstrations
  that training has already taught the model, so wrong candidates never break them. Being
  selective and being safe are the same dial. Leave-one-task-out over the choice of which
  types to keep reaches 11 against a baseline of 15. See scripts/pool_invariants.py and
  archive/notes/invariant-filter-result.md. This is the second independent pricing of the
  theorem's condition for escaping, after v7.5a. Checks derived from the demonstrations are
  noisier than the model's own beliefs, and this time it showed up offline, so the pattern
  isn't a fluke of one hidden run.


3. NEW NUMBERS FROM THAT DAY'S POOL CENSUS

On the 60-output slice:

- The truth is in the pool for 19 outputs and kgmon gets 15 of them into its top two, so four
  leak at selection.
- The bound from Appendix F allows 18 of 60. Only one leaked output, 3dc255db_0 with three
  dominating candidates among thirteen, is blocked for every monotone selector. So the gap
  inside the theorem is three outputs. Earlier wording, that "the dominators dominate and
  selection is at its ceiling", overstated it. The ceiling is real, but we sit three below it.
- Of the 41 outputs where the truth never appears, 7 have a candidate within three cells of
  it (1ae2feb7_1 is just one cell away), 14 have one within ten cells, 6 have no candidate of
  the right shape at all, and the rest are too far gone to repair.
- Six outputs never even produce the right shape. Those are coverage problems, and no
  selection or repair idea touches them.


4. THE LEVERS, RANKED

They're ranked by expected points times how testable they are, divided by cost. Every lever
names what would kill it. The discipline for slots applies to all of them. Measure offline
first, change one factor per submission, and run the control before porting anything (see
score-push-plan).

---- A. OFFLINE NOW, NO GPU ----

A1. A tuned monotone selector aimed at the three-output gap.
    Killed on 1 August, see section 2. Leave-one-task-out lost one against a bar of two. The
    selection headroom is real (a bound of 18 against 15 attained), but it belongs to A2.

A2. Killed on 4 August, see section 2. Kept here as a record of the design.
    Filtering by mined invariants, which is the theorem's escape through evidence from the
    task. Mine candidate invariants from the demonstrations, such as how the output size maps
    from the input, how the palette maps, how cell counts per colour relate, how component
    counts relate, the symmetry class and the background colour. Keep only invariants that
    hold on every demonstration pair, and kill pool candidates that break one. This reads
    the task rather than the model's beliefs, so the ceiling doesn't apply, and it could
    unseat dominating candidates that kgmon can't.
    The test harness takes the challenges JSON and the pool dump and measures three things.
    The rate of killing true answers among the 19 has to be zero. It counts dominating
    candidates killed on the leaked outputs. And it measures how many kgmon gets after
    filtering.
    Cost was one to two days offline. It dies if it kills any true answer, or if no
    dominating candidate dies.

A3. Add a per-cell margin to the dump on the next rig run.
    The repair question, about the 7 outputs within three cells, can't be settled from the
    current dump because it has no per-cell probabilities, and section 2 shows that
    consensus across the pool can't stand in for them. One extra field per candidate, its
    smallest per-cell margin and which cells have it, decides whether targeted repair is
    alive. That repair would try second-choice colours at the k least confident cells and
    rescore. This is for a decision only, not for deployment.

---- B. ALREADY RUNNING ----

B1. First the dfsbench verdict, then the unmodified LB33 fork as a control, then v7.6, which
    is v7.2 plus the perfpatch plus a 1200-second cap. The order is fixed in
    score-push-plan. The finding in 1d gives the patch a concrete reason to help, which is
    removing sync stalls rather than saving arithmetic.

---- C. SCHEDULING, SMALL CODE, CONFIRMED MECHANISM ----

C1. Divide the time fairly across the views.
    Replace the flat 540 seconds per call with the remaining time divided by the calls left,
    so all 16 views decode something within the task's limit. Section 1e shows that late view
    batches vanish without a trace, which corrupts multiplicity on exactly the long tasks
    where selection is hardest. It's a few lines of code. First we have to log how often the
    batch skipping fires in a replay on the rig. If it's rare the lever is dead, and if it's
    common this is the cheapest real change we have.

C2. Stop early when every view agrees. If all the decoded views give the same greedy answer,
    stop searching that output and bank the time. This can be checked offline from timing
    data before any deployment.

C3. Move spare time between tasks. Bank the time saved by tasks that finish early and give it
    to tasks whose search was cut off while its frontier was still above the floor. Real
    points live here, in the hard tasks that get truncated, but it means rewriting the
    scheduler, so only do it after C1 proves the bookkeeping.

---- D. THE SEARCH ITSELF, THE BIG ENGINEERING ----

D1. Replace depth-first order with a global best-first frontier.
    In this domain NLL builds up almost only at genuine branch points, because most tokens are
    forced. So a priority queue over open nodes, keyed by cumulative NLL, emits candidates in
    exactly descending order of probability, like Dijkstra on a graph with non-negative edges.
    Under any time limit the set it emits is the best possible. Depth-first search instead
    loses arbitrary subtrees, which is exactly the failure we see, zero to three candidates
    with the search truncated on the hard tasks. The engineering cost is managing the cache
    when the frontier jumps around. One option is a priority queue over shallow roots with
    depth-first search below some depth. Another is recomputing on each pop with batched
    prefixes. This is the flagship lever if B1's control run backs the LB33 direction, and it
    combines with everything else.

D2. Speculative stepping with the 2B as the draft model.
    The 2B proposes the forced stretches, the 4B checks them in one batched forward pass, and
    the search only branches where the 4B's probability spreads below the floor. That
    multiplies nodes per second on top of D1 and D3. First measure how often the 2B and 4B
    agree on tokens on fresh RE-ARC, which is cheap now that the fresh set exists. The 2B's
    contamination on public eval doesn't stop it being used on the hidden set.

D3. Search cheaply, score exactly.
    Walk the tree under an int8 or compiled CUDA-graph version of the model, then rescore the
    emitted candidates under the exact bf16 model, so the selection statistics don't change.
    The 0.2 floor tolerates small noise in the logits during the walk, because rescoring
    restores exactness. It works on Kaggle and separates speed from accuracy.

D4. A floor that adapts.
    When the tree finishes before the time limit (num_alive_beams == 0, which line 109 already
    signals), rerun the same view with a floor of 0.10 to widen a pool that's small by design,
    to at most ten candidates. When it's getting cut off, tighten toward 1.2 so it finishes
    the most probable region. It's a control loop around existing code, with no new search.

---- E. THE TRAINING SIDE ----

E1. A meta-learned starting point for the adapter.
    Pre-train a rank-256 LoRA starting point on training episodes sampled from the ARC
    training split, simulating test time, and ship it as the starting point for each task.
    Reaching the same loss in fewer steps frees decode time for C and D. Validate it with the
    restart-probe harness, using loss curves by step, before any submission.

E2. Weight the training loss toward early answer tokens, since the search dies at early
    branch points. Speculative, and it can ride on E1's harness.

---- F. COVERAGE ----

F1. Repair aimed at uncertain cells, which depends entirely on A3's margin dump.

F2. Choosing which demonstrations to keep at decode time. On big tasks cut_to_len drops
    training pairs from the front (line 375), so the model predicts with fewer
    demonstrations exactly where tasks are hardest. Choose which pairs to keep by the adapted
    model's leave-one-out loss instead of by position.

F3. Validate 2B rescoring on fresh pools.
    The theorem's second escape died because it was validated on contaminated data, not
    because the mechanism failed. Build pools on fresh RE-ARC tasks, using the 120 minted
    that day, and rescore them with the 2B. If it lifts the truth there, deploy it, because
    neither model is contaminated on the hidden set. This brings a named escape route back
    with a clean place to test it.


4b. THE REAL CONSTRAINT AND WHAT IT MEANS (corrected 1 August)

The constraint that binds is one submission a day. It isn't the GPU quota.

Here's why the quota doesn't bind. Kaggle only sets KAGGLE_IS_COMPETITION_RERUN when it
re-runs a notebook to score it. On our own commit the variable isn't set, so the notebook
reads the public evaluation set instead of the hidden one, and it can queue a few tasks
instead of all of them. LB33 limits itself to four keys (0934a4d8, 36a08778, 981571dc and
aa4ec2a5), and its commit finished in about 15 minutes. The full 240-task run happens inside
the scoring rerun, which uses Kaggle's compute rather than our weekly allowance.
arc_solver.py line 258 already switches the data file in rerun_mode. Check that it also
shrinks the queue on commit.

That leaves about 90 submissions before the main-track deadline on 2 November, with roughly a
day of delay per experiment.

This reverses the earlier advice in this file. Don't bundle changes. Bundling was a response
to a scarcity that wasn't real. With about 90 slots there's room to change one factor at a
time, and a confounded result costs more days untangling afterwards than a clean one costs to
run. The bundling rule from an earlier draft, one open-ended change plus some bounded ones, is
retired.

The rule for submissions is never to spend a day on a variant unless it has both of these.

  (a) an offline reason, from the rig, to expect it to work, and
  (b) exactly one changed factor against a named baseline that has already been scored.

A variant without (a) is a guess and belongs on the rig first. A variant without (b) can't be
attributed and wastes a day of information.

The rule against starving the queue is that one submission a day means a day with nothing
queued is lost for good. The rig's job is to keep the queue full. An empty queue with an idle
rig is the real emergency, not a slow experiment.


5. WHAT I WOULD ACTUALLY DO, IN ORDER

Two tracks run side by side. The submission queue uses one slot a day and must never be empty.
The build track runs on the rig, costs no slots and exists to keep the queue fed.

THE SUBMISSION QUEUE, one factor each, each against a named baseline.

  S1  The LB33 control, an unmodified fork. Committed on 1 August and waiting to submit. It
      has no baseline, because it's the reference for whether 33.89 transfers at all.

  S2  v7.2 with the cap at 1200 seconds instead of 1800, and nothing else changed. The
      baseline is v7.2 at 30.97. The history here is more tangled than it looks. The paper
      had withdrawn the cap hypothesis because the public build forks a source that
      hardcodes 1800, but that was checked against the wrong notebook. I pulled
      mikelou1/arc-agi2-lb33-89-minimal-perfpatch on 1 August, and its own decode loop has
      a literal 1200 on the path that actually runs (line 851 of the flattened source). So
      the hypothesis is back, and the paper has been corrected.
      Against it, v7.4 already moved the cap to 1200 and lost 1.53 points. But that run also
      fired 24 greedy fills nobody budgeted for, through a check that treated the string "0"
      as true, so two things changed and the run is void rather than evidence against. Our
      timing model says four tasks past 1200 seconds used 26.3% of the budget and returned
      nothing, which predicts the change is neutral or better. It's a genuinely open
      one-factor question and worth a day.

  S3  Done on 2 August. v7.5a scored 27.92 against v7.2's 30.97, so the AIF prune costs about
      seven task credits. Whether that's harm to selection, a wrong expected count demoting
      correct candidates, or harm to time, the executor pushing the end of the queue past
      the twelve-hour limit, is settled by the count of decoded tasks the last cell now
      prints. Read it in the Kaggle interface.
      For the theory, the ceiling theorem names running a program against the
      demonstrations as a way past the selection bound. This was the first time we ran that
      kind of check, and it made things worse. The escape needs a check that's more accurate
      than the model's own beliefs, and a noisy one is worse than none. That's a real result
      for the paper. It doesn't refute the theorem, it prices its precondition.

  S3b Done on 3 August. The replicate scored 30.56 against the original's 30.97, a
      difference of exactly one task credit. Three runs of the same method (v7.1 at 30.56,
      v7.2 at 30.97 and the replicate at 30.56) give a mean of 30.70, a range of 0.41 and a
      standard deviation of 0.24. That suggested run-to-run noise was about one credit
      rather than several points, and the consequences looked large.
        - Every regression after v7.2 would be real, between 4.7 and 11.2 standard deviations
          below the lowest baseline run, and our changes would have been harmful.
        - The wall-clock explanation for the bimodal scores would be unsupported, since a
          hard time limit with variable session speed should spread these three runs and it
          didn't.
        - The instrument would be trustworthy at the level of one credit, so a lever would
          have to move the score by more than about 0.4 to be seen.
      I kept a caveat at the time that three runs is a small sample. That caveat turned out
      to matter. Later runs of the same artifact spread over 4.17 points, which reversed all
      three conclusions above.

  S4  v7.2 without the discarded AIF work. This is the strongest remaining candidate and the
      only one that's nearly free. The v7.2 that scores 30.5 to 31.0 starts a
      ThreadPoolExecutor and runs run_aif_analysis on every task, then throws the result
      away and overwrites it with a dummy. The arm was switched off by discarding its output,
      not by skipping its work. The current builder only starts it when ARC_AIF isn't
      "off", so a rebuilt v7.2 never pays the cost. That rebuild has never been scored. It
      hands back per-task time spent on something that gets thrown away anyway, so unlike
      every lever above it removes work rather than adding it. Removing work is the
      direction that has actually helped us before, as with stripping the AIF arm and
      restoring the batching. The baseline is the replicate at 30.56, from the same week and
      environment.

  S5  v7.2 plus whichever of S2 and S3 won, together. That's the first deliberate
      two-factor run, and only because both parts will have their own numbers by then.

  S6 onward, fed by the build track below, in whatever order the results justify.

If a slot would otherwise go empty, re-run the current best configuration unchanged. A
replicate measures the run-to-run noise in the hidden score, which we've never measured and
which every comparison above quietly assumes.

THE BUILD TRACK, on the rig only, costing no slots, roughly in order of expected value.

  B1  Done, and it was real. Our notebook only shrinks the commit queue when ARC_N_EVAL is
      above zero (line 1507). The config block sets ARC_N_EVAL to "40" at line 30 and then
      overrides it to "0" at line 39, and 0 means all 120. So every interactive commit has
      been running the full public evaluation set. LB33 queues four hardcoded keys, and its
      commit finished in about 15 minutes. The fix is to set ARC_N_EVAL to a small number in
      the committed config. It costs nothing and gives back hours on every iteration.

  B2  A2, invariant mining. The lead offline item, now that A1 is dead. It carries the
      selection headroom (a bound of 18 against 15 attained), and it's paper material as the
      theorem's escape made concrete.

  B3  The measurement C1 needs first. How often does the task limit silently drop view
      batches 8 to 15? If it's often, C1 is the cheapest real change we have.

  B4  F3, 2B rescoring on fresh pools. The audit showed the 2B is genuinely capable, at 65% on
      fresh data, and contaminated only on public eval, so the hidden set is fair ground for
      it. Validate on fresh pools and then queue it.

  B5  D4, the floor that adapts, a control loop around existing code.

  B6  D1, the best-first frontier search. The flagship engineering item, and the one most
      likely to move us by more than a point. Start it once S1 and S2 have told us whether
      search throughput is really where the score lives.

What I'm not doing is anything in section 2, any submission that fails the rule in 4b, any
use of agents, or trusting a number shown on the leaderboard as a measurement of the code
published beside it.
