\begin{abstract}
We run the 2025 ARC Prize winning recipe, a small grid model with per-task test-time training (TTT)
and augment-infer-reverse-vote (AIRV) selection, on a single 16~GB consumer GPU, and ask what actually
binds at that scale. The binding constraint is generation throughput, not model quality and not memory.
Moving decode off the Hugging Face eager path onto llama.cpp buys a $13\times$ speedup, turns an
overnight failure-prone evaluation into a sub-90-minute one, and is what makes per-task TTT tractable
at all, by hot-swapping each task's adapter on the fast engine. Building on the NVARC Qwen3-4B grid
model, the system scores \textbf{12.79\%} without TTT on the 120-task public evaluation set. The lift
from TTT depends on regularization. A naive adapter overfits and ties the baseline, while a
regularized, scale-tuned configuration reaches \textbf{17.6\%} on the 84 trainable tasks and lifts the
full evaluation to a blended \textbf{16.28\%}. On the hidden main-track leaderboard the same recipe
scores \textbf{13.61\%} with TTT and 6.53\% without, and the public-to-hidden transfer is itself a
finding. The no-TTT baseline halves, and across the follow-on submissions every candidate-side lever
that lifted or tied the public evaluation regressed the hidden score, so public-eval gains must be
re-validated in the exact deployed condition before they are trusted. The climb then came from the training side rather than the vote. Deepening the
regularized adapter lifted the hidden score to \textbf{18.19\%}, a 4.58-point gain and the first
configuration to beat the shipped operating point. The number is not a wall but climbs under the 16~GB
ceiling as the per-task training deepens, while the methods that reach the field's level, heavy rank-256
test-time training at an eight-thousand-token context, run far slower than the contest budget allows on one
card. That configuration turns out to fit the card's memory once the attention kernel is chosen
deliberately, which corrects an earlier conclusion of our own, so the binding resource is time rather than
capacity. We then measure the gap rather than assert it. Reproducing one such public pipeline on the
contest's own four-L4 node, and repairing two defects the community port had introduced into it, scores
\textbf{30.64\%} averaged over eight runs of that one configuration, with a best single run of
\textbf{32.64\%}. That result is a reproduction of named public work and is not a single-card result,
and we report it as the measured price of the constraint, roughly twelve points against the best
configuration we could serve inside the single-card budget.

We quote a mean rather than a best because of what those six runs turned out to be. One immutable
Kaggle kernel version, submitted five times, scored \textbf{30.56, 32.64, 28.47, 31.39 and 30.56} on
identical code: a spread of \textbf{4.17 points}, ten task credits. The full family has a standard
deviation of 1.18 points. Measured against it, \emph{every} build this project called a regression, and both
public notebooks we ran as controls, fall inside two standard deviations. That is the paper's most
transferable result. At this operating point a single hidden-set score does not resolve the method
differences the field routinely reports, our own included, and we only found it because we spent
submissions re-running one unchanged artifact.

Past that point the work becomes an account of what it takes to trust a number at all in this setting,
and four results carry more than the accuracies do. A public notebook \emph{displaying} 33.89 scored
\textbf{28.06} when we forked it unmodified and submitted it ourselves, which is below our own build;
we had spent a week producing three quantified explanations of a gap that did not exist, and we report
them in the order we believed them. The selection ceiling turns out to be closeable by proof rather
than by sweep: on a dumped candidate pool, the correct grid is dominated in every recorded statistic
for most of the outputs that leak, so no monotone rule over those statistics can recover them and the
family of sixteen reweightings we were about to test was refuted before it ran. A scheduling
hypothesis that the reclaimed time budget could fund restarts measured a 4.4-point gain and was
\textbf{nothing}: rerunning the same seed disagrees with itself as often as different seeds disagree
with each other, so the apparent effect was the decoder's own arithmetic, and only a same-seed
replicate arm separated the two. And the residual is capability rather than budget, since 62\% of tasks
never place the correct grid in the candidate pool under any of sixteen views while consuming 69\% of
the compute. We give an honest accounting of
what the recipe costs and where the effort should go for anyone working with one consumer GPU rather
than the winner's hardware. The contest itself provides four L4 GPUs, 96~GB together, where the
offline frontier now leads near 66\% on the strength of large-scale synthetic-data pretraining, so we read the 16~GB
result as the efficient floor of a larger climb, and the ceiling in our title is the 16~GB card itself, the hardware wall that shapes every choice here and not a limit on the task. All code, the
conversion recipe, and a development log of every measurement are open source under MIT-0.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Efficient abstraction is the point}
ARC-AGI-2~\cite{chollet,arcagi2} is a benchmark of abstraction and reasoning. Each task gives a few
input/output grid pairs that demonstrate a transformation, and the solver must apply that
transformation to a held-out input. The tasks are designed to be easy for humans and hard for
machines, and the competition is run \emph{offline}, which rules out frontier API models and forces
every entrant to ship a self-contained system. The 2025 ARC Prize was won by NVARC at 24.0\% on
ARC-AGI-2 using a 4B grid model with per-task TTT~\cite{arc2025,nvarc}. The same recipe reproduced
across the top teams. They fine-tune a small model on augmented grid data, train a fresh low-rank
adapter on each test task, then sample many augmented candidates and vote.

The recipe is effective but front-loaded with compute. The base models are pre-trained on clusters of
H100 or GH200 GPUs, though the winning offline submission itself runs on four L4 GPUs. Most researchers do not have that hardware, and the ARC Prize's stated mission is
\emph{efficient} skill acquisition, not skill bought with compute. We therefore characterize the
recipe under a constraint we think is widely shared, a single RTX~5070~Ti with 16~GB, on the
Blackwell architecture, running native Windows. The aim on one card is not to top the leaderboard but to build a working, reproducible system, measure
honest numbers, and map where the small model stops being enough. That map is the efficient floor of a
larger climb. The ceiling in the title is the hardware, 16~GB of VRAM, not a score, and the number climbs beneath it as the method deepens, from 13.61 to 18.19 on the hidden set (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We also put a number on the ceiling itself. Reproducing a public pipeline that cannot fit one card, on the hardware the contest provides, averages 30.64\% over eight runs on the same hidden set and peaks at 32.64\%, so the constraint is worth roughly twelve points rather than an unknown amount. The same contest hands every entrant four L4 GPUs, 96~GB together, and the offline frontier
on that hardware has already passed 50\% with refinement loops and program synthesis~\cite{arc2025}. We
read the 16~GB walls not as the limit of the task but as the exact places scaling up has to break, and
Section~\ref{sec:scale} lays out that path. Figure~\ref{fig:solve} shows a representative task our system
solves end to end.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item A reproducible offline ARC-AGI-2 system for the custom-vocabulary NVARC model on a single
16~GB card, with a clean \textbf{12.79\%} no-TTT baseline (Section~\ref{sec:results},
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
\item The finding that, on this hardware class, the inference \emph{engine} dominates every other
knob, since moving decode off the Hugging Face eager path onto llama.cpp is worth a $13\times$ speedup
(Section~\ref{sec:engine}).
\item A practical bridge that makes per-task TTT runnable on one card by hot-swapping GGUF adapters
on the fast engine, and a measured, regularization-dependent lift to a \textbf{16.28\%} blend
(Section~\ref{sec:ttt}, \ref{sec:ttt-results}).
\item An ``accuracy per GPU-hour'' accounting that names the three walls a compute-poor practitioner
hits first, the decode ceiling, the VRAM limit on large-grid TTT, and adapter overfitting
(Section~\ref{sec:disc}).
\item The measured finding that public-eval gains do not transfer to the hidden set. The no-TTT
baseline halves from 12.79\% to 6.53\%, and across seven leaderboard submissions every candidate-side
lever that lifted or tied the public evaluation regressed the hidden score when deployed
(Section~\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:lessons}).
\item A reading of why those walls are a property of a small model on one card rather than of the task,
and a concrete path that scales the same verifier, TTT, and selection onto the full 96~GB the contest
provides, where the offline frontier now leads near 66\% (Section~\ref{sec:scale}).
\item A complete open-source release, the conversion patches, the pipeline, the TTT harness, and a
development log of every measurement and dead end behind these numbers (Appendix~\ref{app:log}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{ARC and the measure of intelligence.} ARC was introduced to measure skill-acquisition
efficiency rather than skill itself~\cite{chollet}. ARC-AGI-2~\cite{arcagi2} raises compositional
difficulty while keeping tasks human-solvable, widening the human/machine gap that frontier models
had begun to close on ARC-AGI-1.

\textbf{Test-time training.} The dominant 2025 approach fits a small per-task adapter at inference,
trading test-time compute for generalization. The ARC Prize 2025 technical report~\cite{arc2025}
identifies this, together with heavy synthetic pre-training and disciplined engineering, as the core
of the winning NVARC solution~\cite{nvarc}, whose released model we build on directly. We hold this
recipe fixed and ask what survives a single consumer GPU.

\textbf{Two different things are called test-time training.} The name collides, and the collision
matters for reading anything in this paper against the wider literature. In the ARC line, TTT means
\emph{supervised} fine-tuning on a task's demonstration pairs, which are labelled data the task
supplies; this is the sense used throughout here and the sense the ARC Prize 2024 report
fixes~\cite{arc2024}. In the older line beginning with Sun et al.~\cite{sun2020}, TTT means
\emph{self-supervised} adaptation on the unlabeled test instance itself, with descendants using
entropy minimisation or masked autoencoding~\cite{ttmae}. Akyurek et al.~\cite{akyurek2024}
inherited the ARC-local sense and flagged the difference themselves. The two procedures share a name
and almost nothing else.

The distinction has a consequence we inherit rather than choose. Our pipeline descends from the
ARChitects~\cite{architects2025}, who state that they compute gradients only on output grids, so
that ``the model is never tasked with predicting an input grid''. The test input, the single largest
piece of task-specific information available, therefore reaches our adapter only as a decode-time
prompt and never as a loss target. Whether that is a missed opportunity or a correct design choice
is an empirical question, and Section~\ref{sec:prefit} answers it for our system.

\textbf{Augmentation and voting.} Predicting under the eight dihedral symmetries of the grid,
inverting each prediction back to the original frame, and voting is a standard self-consistency
device for ARC. It is cheap, training-free, and provides a usable confidence signal
(Section~\ref{sec:airv}).

\textbf{Synthetic data.} Procedural generators such as RE-ARC~\cite{rearc} and LLM-mutated corpora
supply the augmented grid data these models are trained on. We use RE-ARC only in a base-model
probe (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), not in the results below.

\textbf{Efficient and on-device inference.} Our engineering contribution, getting a custom-vocabulary
grid transformer to decode near the memory-bandwidth roofline on a consumer card and then serving
per-task adapters on it, sits in the practical lineage of quantized GGUF inference. The novelty is
not the engine but the demonstration that the full TTT recipe, adapter serving included, fits one
16~GB card, and a measurement of exactly where it stops.

\section{System and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model and prompting}
\label{sec:model}
We use the NVARC \texttt{qwen3\_4b\_grids15\_sft139} model, a Qwen3-4B~\cite{qwen3} with a 16-token
vocabulary, the ten grid colours as digits, a newline, two role markers, and three specials
(Appendix~\ref{app:format}). A grid is serialized one token per cell, and a colour value equals its
token id. Because the role and special tokens cannot be produced by tokenizing text, we assemble
every prompt at the token-id level as a ChatML few-shot sequence. Getting this byte-exact is the
single most important correctness condition, since a one-token drift between training and inference
silently destroys accuracy.

\subsection{The engine is the bottleneck}
\label{sec:engine}
Our most consequential finding concerns throughput. Decoding a 4B model is memory-bound. Each token
streams the full weight matrix from VRAM, so for a weight footprint $M$ on a card of bandwidth $B$
the decode rate is bounded by a first-order roofline
\begin{equation}
r_{\max} \;=\; \frac{B}{M}.
\label{eq:roofline}
\end{equation}
With $B=896$~GB/s, Eq.~\eqref{eq:roofline} gives $132$ tok/s in bf16 ($M=6.77$~GB), $249$ in Q8\_0
($M=3.6$~GB), and $444$ in Q4\_K\_M ($M=2.02$~GB). The standard Hugging Face eager path prefills
healthily ($\approx 3{,}400$ tok/s) but \emph{decodes} at only $\approx 13$ tok/s, about a tenth of
the bf16 roof, because of per-token framework overhead with no CUDA graphs. The usual remedy,
compiling the decode step, is unavailable on this platform. \texttt{torch.compile} needs Triton, and
Triton has no native-Windows backend (Appendix~\ref{app:log}).

The fix is to change engines. We convert the model to GGUF and serve it with llama.cpp. The custom
vocabulary needs two small, documented converter patches (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}): the unknown
tokenizer hash is mapped to the \texttt{qwen2} pre-tokenizer it actually uses, and two throwaway
byte-pair merges are added to satisfy a loader that requires a non-empty merge list (they never fire
on grid text). The prebuilt CUDA~12.4 binary runs on the Blackwell card through PTX just-in-time
compilation. Measured decode rises to $102$ tok/s in bf16, $170$ in Q8\_0, and $244$ in Q4\_K\_M
(Figure~\ref{fig:roofline}), a $13\times$ speedup over eager at Q8\_0, which we adopt. It is
near-lossless (14 of 15 training tasks reproduced exactly under greedy decoding) and has a 3.6~GB
footprint. The server's default prompt cache needs one note. It reserves $\approx 680$~MiB per
unique prompt, and since every ARC prompt is unique it yields no reuse and destabilizes sustained
generation, so we disable it and run a single supervised stream.

\subsection{AIRV: augment, infer, reverse, vote}
\label{sec:airv}
Let $f$ be the served model and $\mathcal{G}$ the eight dihedral (D4) symmetries of the grid. For a
test input $x$ we generate one prediction per symmetry, invert it back to the original frame, and
vote. Writing $n_c$ for a candidate's vote count and $\bar{\ell}_c$ for its mean sequence log-prob,
\begin{equation}
c_g \;=\; g^{-1}\!\big(f(g(x))\big),\qquad
\hat{Y} \;=\; \select_{c}\ \big(\,n_c,\ \bar{\ell}_c\,\big),\qquad
n_c=\big|\{g\in\mathcal{G}: c_g=c\}\big|,\quad
\bar{\ell}_c=\tfrac{1}{n_c}\!\!\sum_{g:\,c_g=c}\!\!\log p(c_g),
\label{eq:airv}
\end{equation}
where $\select$ returns the two distinct candidates with the highest vote count, ties broken by mean
log-prob, and emits them as the two attempts the metric allows. The vote count is itself a usable
confidence signal. Agreement across symmetries predicts correctness (Figure~\ref{fig:votes}). The
whole offline system (the trainer, the converter, and the server, which never share VRAM) is sketched
in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\subsection{Per-task test-time training}
\label{sec:ttt}
For TTT we fit a per-task LoRA adapter $\Delta W = s\,BA$ of rank $r$ and scale $s$ on the task's own
train pairs $T$, with a leave-one-out target and the loss masked to the answer tokens:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; -\!\!\sum_{(u,v)\in T}\ \sum_{t}
\log p_{\theta}\big(v_t \mid v_{<t},\, \mathrm{ctx}_{\,T\setminus\{(u,v)\}},\, u\big),
\label{eq:ttt}
\end{equation}
drawing a fresh random augmentation each step. Capping the few-shot context to $\approx 1{,}600$
tokens drops a training step from $\approx 13$~s to $\approx 2$~s, so training is no longer the
expensive part.

The harder problem is \emph{serving} the trained adapter. TTT must run in PyTorch, where decoding is
the slow 13 tok/s and intermittently becomes quadratic after a step. The fast engine does not train.
We bridge them (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}): train in PyTorch, save each adapter, convert it to a GGUF
adapter, and hot-swap it on llama.cpp through its \texttt{/lora-adapters} endpoint, where decode
holds $\approx 115$ tok/s with no stalls. We run three phases (train all, convert all from a clean
process, then serve and generate all) so the two frameworks never share VRAM
(Figure~\ref{fig:phases}). Large-grid tasks remain too VRAM-bound to train and are excluded by their
largest-train-pair token length (Figure~\ref{fig:vram}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Two regimes, and the leaderboard progression}
Two hardware regimes appear in this paper, and every number belongs to exactly one of them. Every
public-eval number, the 12.79\% baseline, the 17.6\% trainable-task peak, and the 16.28\% blend, is
measured on the single RTX~5070~Ti against the 120-task public evaluation set, and all method
development ran there. Every leaderboard number is measured by Kaggle's offline scoring rerun on the
contest's four-L4 node, against the hidden 240-task set (120 semi-private plus 120 private). The
submissions through v6.1 use the four L4s only to shard tasks for throughput, since the bf16 per-task
training does not fit the 12-hour budget on one card. No single task there needs more than one 16~GB
GPU, so the method under test is the single-card recipe. The v7 entries are the one exception and this
paper marks them as such wherever they appear. They reproduce a public pipeline whose per-task training
runs at rank 256 over an eight-thousand-token context, which fits 16~GB only under an attention kernel we
identified after those runs and would still need roughly forty hours on one card against the twelve the
contest allows, so those numbers measure competition hardware rather than the card this work is about. They are kept in the same table
deliberately, because the distance between the best single-card entry and the best four-L4 entry is the
most direct measurement we have of what the constraint costs.

That exclusion is measured rather than assumed. Sweeping LoRA rank against sequence length on the single
card, with training configured exactly as our shipped path, every rank from 32 to 256 fits comfortably at a
two-thousand-token sequence and every one of them fails at four thousand. The blocking term is neither the
adapter nor the optimizer, which together account for under a gigabyte at rank 32 and three at rank 256. It
is activation memory, which grows from roughly 2.6~GB at two thousand tokens to roughly 8.5~GB at four
thousand, a factor of 3.3 for a doubling of length. That superlinear growth is attention materializing its
score matrix. Leaner adapter dtypes buy a full rank doubling at equal memory, enough to run rank 256 at two
thousand tokens in 14.5~GB, but they leave the activation term untouched. On that evidence we recorded the
context as a hard wall and the eight-thousand-token configuration as beyond the card.

That conclusion was wrong, and the sweep above contains the reason. It never chose an attention kernel.
Repeating the measurement with the backend forced rather than left to the framework's dispatch
(Table~\ref{tab:attn}) moves the wall entirely. Under cuDNN attention the rank-256 configuration trains at
an eight-thousand-token sequence in \textbf{14.86~GB} at 5.04 seconds per step, on the same card that had
failed at four thousand. The framework's automatic selection is numerically identical to the forced math
backend at both lengths, because the flash and memory-efficient kernels report no available implementation
for this GPU in this build, so the dispatcher had been falling back to the quadratic path throughout. The
result is corroborated from outside the measurement, since the four-L4 runs fit the same training
configuration into 13.0~GB under a flash-attention kernel, and two different memory-efficient
implementations on two different cards landing within two gigabytes of each other is the agreement we would
expect.

Three limits on that result, stated because they bound what follows from it. It measures training memory
for a single sequence at batch size one, where the deployed pipeline may pack differently. It measures
memory and step time rather than numerical equivalence, which would need a paired run against the math
backend on a real task. And it shows only that the training configuration fits, not that the pipeline runs
end to end on one card, which would additionally require porting that pipeline off a training library with
no working install on this platform.

What it does settle is which resource binds. At 5.04 seconds per step the 128-step schedule is about eleven
minutes for one adapter, so the 240-task contest set would need roughly forty hours against a twelve-hour
budget. The card holds the winning configuration and cannot serve it in time. That is a throughput limit
rather than a capacity limit, and it is the second time in this work that a ceiling we recorded turned out
to be a default rather than a property of the hardware, after the six-hundred-token training truncation of
Section~\ref{sec:biggrid}. Both were found by re-examining a measurement we had already filed as settled.

The scaled single-scale configuration (v2.1) reached \textbf{13.61\%}, ranking 468 of about 4000, and
four candidate-side submissions failed to beat it. One errored in the scored rerun, and each of the three that
scored carried exactly one lever that had lifted or tied the public evaluation, multi-scale voting
with colour permutation, more-candidates sampling, and the white-box plausibility filter, and each
regressed the hidden score (Section~\ref{sec:ablations} gives the levers and the mechanism). The eight
greedy D4 views at a single adapter scale are a local optimum that the candidate and voting side does
not improve, and the repeated public-to-hidden reversal is itself a finding (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
The break came from the other side of the pipeline. A deep-regularized adapter that trains longer at a
higher rank (60 steps, rank 32, Table~\ref{tab:deepttt}), moving the per-task training rather than the
vote, lifted the hidden score to \textbf{18.19\%} in v6.1, a 4.58-point gain and the first configuration
to beat v2.1. Its intermediate form v6 scored 12.36\% as the same recipe crippled by a disk-exhaustion
bug that left 73 of 232 tasks with no adapter, and fixing the scratch path recovered the full lift. This
is the paper's thesis made concrete, the candidate and voting side is saturated and the per-task training
side is where the hidden number moves. For reference the 2025 winner reached 24.0\% with TTT~\cite{arc2025} on the same Qwen3-4B base lineage
with a more developed recipe.

posted and it is not a single-card result, since its per-task training cannot be served on one card inside
the contest's twelve hours. Read against v6.1, the best configuration we ran
within that budget,
the pair gives the cleanest figure in this work. The same author, the same base model, the same hidden
set and the same twelve-hour budget yield \textbf{18.19\%} under 16~GB and \textbf{30.97\%} on the

\paragraph{The replicate, and the error bar every comparison had been assuming.}
Every comparison above silently assumed the hidden score of a fixed configuration is stable, and
nothing in the project had ever measured it. After the fifth consecutive build scored below v7.2, with
the scores split into two clusters and nothing between them, the cheapest explanation left was that
30.97 had been a favourable draw and the regressions were noise, so we spent a submission on the only
experiment that answers it, v7.2 resubmitted byte-identical except for the size of its own commit run.
It scored \textbf{30.56}. On three runs that looked like a range of 0.41, one task credit, and a
standard deviation of 0.24, and we wrote that a lever must move roughly 0.4 points to be
distinguishable from a re-run. Two further runs destroyed that estimate, and the second destroyed it
completely.

The fourth run deleted the solver's dead symbolic-arm computation, work whose output the scoring
v7.2 provably discards on the next line, a change with no path to the computed answers except
timing. It scored \textbf{30.00}. The fifth was the decisive one. We resubmitted the replicate
kernel, and resubmitting a Kaggle kernel version re-runs an immutable artifact: not a rebuild, not a
re-export, the same committed version identifier as the run that had scored 30.56. It scored
\textbf{32.64}. Identical code, two executions, \textbf{2.08 points apart}, which is five task
credits.

We then kept submitting that same kernel version. Five executions of one immutable artifact scored
\textbf{30.56, 32.64, 28.47, 31.39 and 30.56}: a range of \textbf{4.17 points}, ten task credits, on
code that cannot have changed. This is the central measurement of the paper and we arrived at it by
widening the same estimate three times, each time in the same direction.

The same-method family is now eight runs, mean \textbf{30.64}, standard deviation \textbf{1.18},
range 4.17 points. The 95\% interval on that standard deviation is $[0.78, 2.41]$ and has narrowed
with each addition, so the honest statement is that the noise is at least three quarters of a point
and might be two and a half.

The consequence is not a correction to one claim, it is the retirement of a whole class of claim.

Two of those lines are worth stating plainly rather than leaving in a table. \textbf{The LB33 control
scored 28.47, and so did our own unmodified pipeline.} The phantom-notebook section
(Section~\ref{sec:phantom}) concluded that two public artifacts ``both land near 28 when executed,
below our own unchanged pipeline's band''. Our own unchanged pipeline reaches exactly that number.
The conclusion that survives is the one that never depended on the gap, that a displayed score does
not bind to the published file; the comparison we drew underneath it does not survive. And
\textbf{v7.5a, the symbolic prune we treated as the theorem's escape route measured and refuted,
has now been reported at 11, 3.0, 1.93, 2.14 and 2.30 standard deviations as the sample grew from
three runs to eight, without its own score ever changing.} Its verdict crossed the two-sigma line in
both directions on single additional draws, which is a sharper argument against single-comparison
verdicts than anything we could write. The interval on the standard deviation itself is
$[0.78, 2.41]$, so v7.5a's distance from the mean is somewhere between about one and three
sigma and the threshold is not a stable object. We keep the conclusion about oracle accuracy because
the offline invariant-mining experiment reaches it independently and does not rely on the hidden
score, and we decline to reinstate the hidden-score argument on a tenth of a sigma.

This also settles the mechanism question the earlier draft went back and forth on. We wrote that the
wall-clock account failed because three baseline runs did not spread. They spread as soon as the
sample grew, and they spread in the comparison where nothing but session speed could differ. The
decode is deadline-bounded in three places, so how far the machine gets decides where the search
truncates and therefore which candidates ever exist. The throughput account is reinstated and the
alternative explanations of the cluster structure are unnecessary.

We report all of this rather than quietly restating the family because it is the error this paper
spends its discussion section warning other people about, and we committed it three times.
The winners' own writeup remarks in passing that
they ``saw 1-2 points variability in the scores across different re-runs of the same
notebook''~\cite{nvarc}, and their batched decoder is nondeterministic by construction, logits
changing with batch composition. The variability was known inside the strongest team and, as far as
we can find, never measured or accounted for by anyone. Every ablation on this leaderboard,
including theirs, is read against noise nobody had quantified.

What the pair does not do is attribute that gap. The two runs differ in their hardware and in their method
at the same time, so twelve points is the size of the distance rather than an account of where it comes
from. Isolating the method term would mean running the reduced configuration on the four-L4 node rather
than on the single card, holding the platform fixed and moving only the training context, and we flag that
as the experiment this measurement wants next. Running it on our own hardware instead would confound the
answer with the card, the driver and the attention kernel all at once, which is the fault the sweep in
Section~\ref{sec:results} corrected in itself.

The more useful statement is about \emph{where} the constraint acts, and by the end of this work we can
make it precisely rather than as a point count. It does not act at inference. The offline track fixes
that for everyone at four L4 GPUs and twelve hours, our submissions run inside that budget, and the cap
is in fact why the field runs a 4B model at all. It acts at pretraining, which is unconstrained, and the
resource is nameable from the winning entry's own published configuration: a full-parameter supervised
fine-tune under tensor-parallel training across eight GPUs, over a corpus whose synthetic component is
of order $10^6$ puzzles produced by an LLM pipeline that summarizes and recombines task concepts, writes
transformation programs and executes them. We estimate that generation alone at six to eight months of
continuous single-card inference, before any training step. So the ceiling in our title is a training
ceiling wearing an inference costume, and the twelve points measure the distance between a base one card
can fine-tune and a base one card cannot build.

Three consequences follow, and each is a measurement rather than an inference. The published artifacts do
not close the gap: our base \emph{is} that model, and our own earlier continued fine-tune regressed
because two of the datasets we added were already inside its training corpus
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Nor can the gap be closed by generating replacement data, because the tasks
this model misses are not separable from the ones it reaches on any structural axis we can measure,
which is the only kind of property a generator can be instructed to produce
(Section~\ref{sec:restart}). And it cannot be closed from the inference side, since selection is bounded
by a dominance argument on the candidate pool (Section~\ref{sec:selection}) and the scheduling lever
turned out to be numerical noise once a same-seed control was run. Four independent levers, four
independent closures, all pointing at the same unpurchasable resource. That is a sharper claim than
twelve points, and unlike twelve points it is falsifiable: a stronger base model reached on comparable
hardware would refute it.

A third submission of the reproduction, identical to the second except that it leaves the per-task search
cap at the upstream default of 1800 seconds rather than the 1200 we had chosen for margin, scored
\textbf{30.97\%} and placed 264th. Two facts about that number matter more than its size.

Roughly sixty entries on the leaderboard carry it exactly. A tie that wide is not coincidence, it is the
public notebook being forked and submitted unchanged, which identifies 30.97 as that notebook's number
rather than as anyone's result. Our restoration therefore converges on the public baseline exactly rather
than approximately, and that is stronger evidence that the two port defects we isolated account for the
whole difference than the near-agreement we claimed before. It is also, plainly, a reproduction rather than
a differentiating result, and we report it as one.

We first read the 0.41 points between our two runs of it as the per-task cap, since the two builds
differed by nothing else we had declared. That reading was wrong, and its correction is the most useful
thing in this section. An audit of the generated notebooks found the cap knob had never reached the solver
at all. The build script rewrote a guard it expected to find as a local variable comparison, the source
writes the same test inline, the pattern never matched, and so every submission from the first reproduction
onward ran the same hardcoded 1800-second cap while its configuration banner printed whichever value we
had set. The two runs were therefore the identical build at the identical cap, and their 0.41-point spread is
run-to-run variance. We took that 0.41 for the noise floor, and it was only the smallest gap the
sample happened to contain. Resubmitting one immutable kernel version later produced 30.56, 32.64,
28.47 and 31.39 on the same artifact, so the floor is about ten task credits and not one, and every
comparison in this work should be read against the wider figure derived above rather than this one.
Appendix~\ref{app:math} derives the resolution limit such a number instantiates. One further observation follows for
anyone reading the leaderboard rather than the method. A single task credit separates an entire sixty-entry
cluster, so on this board the cheapest available gain is not a better model but a better use of the twelve
hours.

The measurement run's own log then priced that claim. Of its 24821 task-seconds, 71 percent went to tasks
that yielded nothing, no solve and no correct grid anywhere in the candidate pool, and the two longest
tasks each burned to within sight of the 1800-second cap for nothing. One task in the forty trained
normally and then decoded no output at all, a forfeited slot that scales to about four on the public 120
and plausibly eight on the hidden 240. Final training loss predicts these dead tasks weakly but usably at
the extreme, cutting every task whose adapter finished above a loss of 0.05 reclaims 2330 seconds while
touching no task the selector harvests, since the one such task with a correct grid in its pool was being
lost at selection anyway. Three scheduler changes follow, none touching the method. A task whose search
returns nothing falls back to a plain greedy decode with its already-trained adapter, which can only fill
otherwise-empty slots. A task whose training loss ends above the cut serves the cheap greedy answer and
skips the expensive search. And the time both changes reclaim becomes margin for the per-task cap, the one
knob already measured to buy a task credit. The next submission carries these as a scheduling variant of
the unchanged pipeline.

\subsubsection{A number that did not reproduce, and a week spent explaining it}
\label{sec:phantom}
The public board then handed us what looked like a controlled measurement and was not one. We report the
episode in full, including three wrong explanations we published internally along the way, because the
correction cost a week and the general lesson is worth more than the specific result.

A public notebook restoring this same pipeline displayed \textbf{33.89} against our restoration's 30.56.
Taking that at face value, we spent the following week attributing the 3.33-point deficit. First to the
dependency tree, since its kernel metadata mounts the original author's wheel set and model upload where
ours mounts a rebuild whose pins predate the current execution image, and our logs had warned throughout
that compiled extensions were being skipped for a version mismatch. That reading priced the software
environment at 3.33 points and reframed the sixty-entry cluster at 30.97 as a population unknowingly
running a degraded one. Rebasing onto that environment scored \textbf{28.61}. Second to the per-task cap,
after an audit found the cap knob had never reached the solver, on the argument that a looser cap lets
slow tasks eat the wall until the queue tail is dropped unstarted. That rested on the belief that the
public build ran a tighter 1200-second cap; it forks a source that hardcodes 1800, as did every
submission of ours through the wiring fault, so the two sides had agreed on the cap all along. The build
sent to test it scored \textbf{29.44} and moved a second factor besides. Third to two changes of our own
in the solver, deleting the port's symbolic arm and regrouping its two-view search batches into fours,
which a diff read module by module leaves as the only differences that survive into deployment.

Then we forked the public notebook unmodified and submitted it from our own account. It scored
\textbf{28.06}, 2.91 points
below the restoration it was supposed to be beating. We took that as showing the deficit had never
existed, and reported it that way. That reading was itself wrong, and the way it was wrong is the most
useful thing in this section.

The notebook we forked was not the notebook the score belonged to. Enumerating the competition's public
notebooks, which are published precisely to be read, turns up one titled for the score in question and
carrying it in its own name, along with a byte-identical fork of it. That notebook runs the same base
model and the same dependency tree as ours, and differs from our best submission in exactly two
respects, both of which govern how much search fits inside the budget. It computes the decoder's
per-node score on the GPU over the twelve grid tokens rather than as a full-vocabulary softmax moved to
the CPU, which is algebraically the same quantity and so cannot change what an unbounded search finds,
but the search is bounded and the cheaper form covers more of the tree per second. And it caps each task
at 1200 seconds rather than 1800. Every other difference we had agonized over is absent: it removes the
symbolic arm and uses the four-view batching, both of which we had arrived at independently.

So the correct account is neither the one we started with nor the one we replaced it with. The number
was real. A public artifact producing it was available for the whole period. We measured a different
artifact, concluded the effect was illusory, and wrote that up with more confidence than testing one
file supports. The 28.06 measurement stands and is worth keeping, since that notebook demonstrably does
not produce the number attached to it in our reading of the board; what does not stand is the inference
from one such test to the claim that nothing produces it.

The exact size stays uncertain and we decline to sharpen it beyond what the design supports. The control
carries the public author's own attachments, so against our 30.97 it moves both the code and the
environment and reads $+2.91$; against our 28.61, which shares its environment and cap, it reads $+0.55$
but that build carried two scheduler additions of its own. Both comparisons favour the restoration and
neither isolates it. One reading does invert cleanly: our own environment now looks better than the one
we had priced at $+3.33$, since every build we ran on the public author's mounts scored below every build
we ran on ours. We state that as a direction, not a number, having just been taught what happens when a
single uncontrolled comparison is promoted to a price.

The identified artifact was then run as its own control, forked byte-identical with only the ownership
metadata changed and mounting its author's original wheel tree and model upload exactly as its metadata
specifies. It scored \textbf{28.47}. That is the second public notebook displaying a 33.89-class number
to land near 28 when actually executed from a clean account, and it converts the episode from an
anecdote about one notebook into a pattern about the board.

\textbf{One sentence of what follows has since been withdrawn, and we leave the reasoning visible
rather than editing it out.} We wrote that both controls landed ``below our own unchanged pipeline's
band'', and used that gap to refute the environment hypothesis outright. Our own unchanged pipeline
subsequently scored \textbf{28.47} on a later execution of an immutable kernel version
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), which is the control's number exactly. So the gap does not exist and no
comparison of single runs at this separation can be made. What survives is the part that never
depended on it: a displayed score binds to a submission and not to the published file, the two are
mutable independently, and forking the artifact unmodified is the only way to learn what it does. The
environment hypothesis is therefore \emph{unsupported} rather than refuted, which is where it stood
before we over-read the control, and the per-task cap's motivation was independently removed by the
clean one-factor test of Appendix~\ref{app:math}. A rig benchmark of the remaining mechanism, the GPU per-node
arithmetic, measured 1.33$\times$ the candidates of the CPU form at a fixed budget with an identical
truth-in-pool count of 1 of 7, so the stated search-throughput mechanism is at best weakly present on
our hardware and bought nothing that scores.

The durable lesson survives both errors and is sharper for them. A score displayed beside a public
notebook is not a measurement of the code in front of you, and it does not even identify which code it
came from. A submission binds to a notebook version and its attachments at submit time, both mutable
afterwards and neither auditable, and a leaderboard entry names a team rather than a file. We treated a
number as though it pointed at an artifact, first believing it and then disbelieving it, when what it
actually required was finding the artifact. The remedy is not scepticism, which produced our second
error, but enumeration: the competition publishes its notebooks, and reading them is both permitted and
the only way to know what a number refers to. We did that a week late, and it took an afternoon.

The costs are worth stating plainly, since they are the argument for the discipline rather than
decoration on it. Believing the number bought three quantified explanations of a gap, one of them
elevated to a claim that the software environment outweighed every method lever we had measured.
Disbelieving it on one control bought a confident published assertion that the effect was illusory.
Enumerating the notebooks bought a two-line mechanism, testable offline, that neither belief would ever
have produced. Roughly sixty leaderboard entries share our 30.97 exactly, which is what forking public
notebooks produces; the same practice means the artifact behind almost any number is sitting in public,
and the reason to look is that it is cheaper than any amount of reasoning about the number.

The methodological cost is measurable in this document. Three published explanations, two of them
quantified to two decimal places, one of them elevated to a central claim about software environments
being worth more than method, all of them explaining a gap that a single control retired in one
submission. The control was cheap and available on day one. We ran it seventh. Each explanation was
individually reasonable and collectively they formed a research programme aimed at a phantom, which is
the failure mode this section exists to record: we never tested whether the thing we were explaining was
real. Section~\ref{sec:lessons} takes it up as the project's sharpest instance of a recurring habit.

Three builds are staged to price this, each moving one factor at the 1800-second cap both sides ran.
One restores the object-count signal alone and keeps our batching, one reverts the batching alone, and
one runs both factors upstream and so tests our build process rather than the method. Section
\ref{sec:limits} records that none of the three has returned at the time of writing, and the prediction
registered before they run is that the symbolic signal carries most of the gap. Appendix~\ref{app:math}
gives the allocation model that the withdrawn cap argument rested on, which remains correct as
mathematics and is now simply attached to no observed effect.

\subsection{Ablations on scale, colour, candidates, filtering, and the big-grid wall}
\label{sec:ablations}
Five further levers, each measured on the 84 trainable tasks or the full evaluation (full per-scale
numbers in Appendix~\ref{app:results}). The adapter \emph{scale} matters. Accuracy peaks at 17.6\% at
scale $1.25$, then declines (Figures~\ref{fig:results},~\ref{fig:flip}). Colour permutation lifts the
no-TTT baseline to 14.4\% but does \emph{not} stack with the already colour-robust adapter. Forcing
TTT onto the big-grid tasks does not help. We recorded the VRAM exclusion as a genuine ceiling rather than
a missed opportunity, a conclusion Section~\ref{sec:biggrid} re-examines, since that early run trained under
a 600-token context cap that admits a single demonstration on these tasks. The fourth lever is how many candidates the vote sees, and it cuts both ways. Sampling each of
the eight D4 views four times raises the no-TTT score from 12.79\% to 13.95\% on the full evaluation, but
like colour permutation it does not stack with the per-task adapter. Carried into a TTT submission those
extra sampled candidates regressed the hidden score from 13.61\% to 13.19\%, and stacking multi-scale voting
and colour permutation regressed it further to 12.78\%. The adapter already concentrates the model on its
single best answer, so more candidates help only the unsure no-TTT base and dilute the confident TTT one.

The fifth lever is a white-box plausibility filter adapted from a small-model recipe~\cite{longt5arc},
which drops candidates that violate the task's colour palette or the train pairs' size pattern before
the vote. On the public evaluation it is exactly neutral. The eight greedy D4 candidates are already
plausible, so filtering changes no attempt on any of the 120 tasks, and where its inferred size rule
misfires, on tasks whose train pairs coincidentally share one output size while the true rule varies,
it empties the candidate list and the pipeline falls back to the unfiltered vote. Submitted as v5, it
regressed the hidden score from 13.61\% to 12.78\%, a roughly two-task loss consistent with that
size-rule tail landing on hidden tasks where the filter kept a plausible wrong candidate instead of
emptying, though run-to-run TTT variance cannot be excluded from outside. The asymmetry is the lesson.
A lever that measures neutral on the public set has no upside and only tail risk on the hidden one.

The eight greedy D4 views at a single scale are a local optimum that neither more votes nor
vote filtering beats. The best blend, TTT at scale $1.25$ where trainable and no-TTT elsewhere,
reaches \textbf{16.28\%} on the full 120-task evaluation, up from 12.79\%. The honest summary is that
naive per-task TTT \emph{ties} a strong no-TTT AIRV baseline, while a regularized, scale-tuned TTT
\emph{beats} it by a consistent if modest margin.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Five lessons that generalize}
\label{sec:lessons}
First, on a single GPU the inference engine is the dominant design choice. Quantization and sampling
mattered far less than the $13\times$ we recovered by moving decode off the eager path. Second, the
real obstacle for TTT is the \emph{serving bridge}. Training needs a gradient framework, fast
generation needs an optimized engine, and per-task TTT produces a fresh adapter the engine must
serve. We show this bridge is buildable on one card, and that once it exists the open question is not
plumbing but \emph{regularization}, because the small per-task training set overfits easily. Third, a
4B model is bimodal on these tasks. It solves or it misses with little partial credit, so the
refinement family that defines the current frontier needs an external partial-credit signal to work at
this scale. A method that only re-ranks the model's own outputs stalls (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Fourth, public-eval gains must be re-validated in the exact deployed condition, because they do not
transfer on their own. The level drops, the no-TTT system halves from 12.79\% public to 6.53\% hidden.
More telling, the deltas reverse. The three candidate-side levers that lifted or tied the public
evaluation, colour permutation with multi-scale voting, more-candidates sampling, and the plausibility
filter, each regressed the hidden TTT score when submitted (Table~\ref{tab:versions}). Two mechanisms
compound. A lever validated on the no-TTT base meets a different system once the adapter has sharpened
the vote, and a lever that measures neutral on one task distribution still carries tail risk on
another. The operational rule this bought is that a lever counts as validated only in the exact
submission condition, and a lever that measures neutral is never shipped.

That rule has one clean counterexample and it sharpens the rule rather than weakening it. Repairing the
community port's two defects was worth eleven points on the public slice and carried to the hidden set in
the same direction (Section~\ref{sec:phantom}). What transferred was a repair, the removal of a component
that was actively discarding tasks and a batching change inside the search. What failed to transfer, every
time, was a lever that re-weighted selection among candidates the system already had. The distinction is
the same one Section~\ref{sec:selection} reaches from the pool statistics, and it is a more useful
predictor than public magnitude: ask whether the change fixes something broken or tunes something working.

Fifth, and most expensive here, confirm that the effect you are explaining exists before you explain it.
A public notebook displaying a score three points above ours produced, over one week, three quantified
explanations of the gap, one of which we elevated to a central claim that the software environment
outweighed every method lever we had measured. Forking that notebook unmodified and submitting it scored
2.91 points \emph{below} our own build (Section~\ref{sec:phantom}). There was no gap. The control was
the cheapest experiment available and we ran it seventh, after the explanations, because each explanation
felt like progress in a way that re-measuring the baseline did not. Two properties of a research question
should now trigger the control first: the effect comes from an artefact whose provenance we cannot audit,
and the effect is the premise for a programme of work rather than one comparison. Both held from day one.
The general form is that a number is evidence about the thing that produced it, and a leaderboard score
beside a public notebook is produced by a submission whose code and inputs are not the ones on display.

\subsection{Accuracy per GPU-hour}
We argue the right framing for compute-poor ARC work is \textbf{accuracy per GPU-hour}
(Figure~\ref{fig:cost}), and the map of walls reported here (the decode ceiling, the VRAM limit on
large-grid TTT, and the overfitting of naive adapters, all in Figure~\ref{fig:levers}) is a first step
toward it. The findings tell a practitioner with similar hardware exactly which problems to solve
first. Fix the engine before touching anything else, budget VRAM by largest-train-pair length, and
regularize the per-task adapter rather than training it harder.

\subsection{Limitations and ongoing work}
\label{sec:limits}
Our absolute accuracy is below NVARC's 24.0\% winning result, by design. That score was set offline on
four L4 GPUs, not a cluster, by a more developed recipe on the same Qwen3-4B base lineage, so the gap
reflects recipe sophistication (synthetic data, ensembling, TTT scale) more than hardware. We study the
one-card inference-plus-TTT recipe rather than maximize a system.
The public-to-hidden gap is a finding, not a caveat (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}). Levels drop, the
no-TTT system carries 12.79\% public to 6.53\% hidden while the TTT blend carries 16.28\% to 13.61\%,
and deltas reverse, with every public-validated candidate-side lever regressing when submitted
(Table~\ref{tab:versions}). The public ARC-AGI-2 eval is the set every open recipe is tuned against,
ours included, so the leaner the recipe the more of its public accuracy is public-specific. Part of
the v1 drop may also be per-task failures in the scored rerun, which the later submissions instrument
with a per-task log, and the TTT system's smaller gap is consistent with both readings.
The 2025 analysis names verifier-guided selection and a refinement loop (check each candidate against
the train pairs, then re-prompt on the discrepancy) as the central advance, so we built and tested it.
It does not lift here. A passive form (weighting the augmentation vote by leave-one-out train fidelity)
and an active form (verification-gated re-sampling on the tasks the base is unsure of) each match the
plain no-TTT vote exactly on the eval slices we ran. The reasons are structural. The verifier is silent
exactly where it is needed, since on a task the base misses it reproduces none of the held-out train
pairs, so there is nothing to select between or correct toward. Our 16-token model also cannot read a
natural-language critique, so the canonical re-prompt-with-the-discrepancy step is not even expressible. We also probed whether a one-time continued LoRA fine-tune of the base on official ARC-AGI-2 training
data and RE-ARC~\cite{rearc} generations yields a better static model. It did not. The fine-tuned base
scores 11.63\% no-TTT against the stock base's 12.79\%, so we ship the stock base. We recorded that at
the time as evidence that more public data is not a free lever once a base is task-tuned, which was the
right conclusion for the wrong reason, and the correct reason is worth more.

Reading the base model's own published training configuration settles it. Our base is the product of a
full supervised fine-tune of a reasoning-tuned Qwen3-4B over a corpus whose named components include
both the official ARC-AGI-2 training split and RE-ARC generations, alongside a synthetic set of order
$10^6$ puzzles built by an LLM pipeline that summarizes puzzle concepts, mixes them into hybrids,
generates transformation code and executes it. We had therefore fine-tuned the model on two datasets it
had already consumed. The 1.16-point regression is duplicate-corpus overfitting rather than a statement
about data scale, and the general lesson is narrower and more useful than the one we drew: before
adding data to a task-tuned base, read what the base was trained on, because the failure mode of
training on a subset of your own pretraining corpus looks exactly like the failure mode of the data
being useless.

The same document bounds what a single card can do about this from above. That fine-tune ran as a full
update under tensor-parallel training across eight GPUs, not as an adapter, so reproducing or extending
the base is not a matter of patience on one 16~GB card. The only base-side lever that remains open to us
is data the published corpus does not already contain, which means running that concept-mixing pipeline
ourselves, and we state it as an option we have scoped rather than one we have tested.

Building on that null result, we tested an expert-iteration self-training loop. We solve the official
training tasks with the current model, distil the base on a curriculum of its own verified-correct
solutions, and iterate. Exact-match grids impose a sharp constraint. A verified-correct solution on a
training task simply is its ground-truth output, so a naive self-training corpus coincides with the
data the continued fine-tune already used. We hoped to escape this through the curriculum, distilling
only the near-miss tasks the base reaches but does not solve robustly, but a 200-task harvest shows
that set is nearly empty. The base self-solves 194 of 200 training tasks under some augmentation
(97\%) and 191 by the robust vote (95.5\%), leaving only three near-miss tasks. The base is saturated
on its own training distribution, so self-training has no signal to add. The training tasks split into
already-solved (nothing to learn) and unsolved (no correct trace), with almost nothing between. This
is a second, sharper negative result on weight refinement, and it explains the fine-tune failure
directly. On one GPU, with a base already tuned to the grid format, neither continued fine-tuning nor
self-training moves ARC-AGI-2, because the public training data carries no signal the base has not
already absorbed.

These three null results, the fine-tune, self-training, and the refinement loop, share one cause. The
base is bimodal on ARC. It solves a task robustly or misses it entirely, with almost no
partial-understanding middle (of the training tasks it harvests, 95.5\% are solved robustly and only
1.5\% are near-misses). Every method that refines, re-ranks, or distils the model's own understanding
therefore has nothing to grip on the tasks it misses, and per-task TTT, the one lever that helped, is
the exception that proves the rule. It does not refine partial understanding. Instead it manufactures new
capability for the task. The implication for compute-poor ARC is concrete. The next lever must supply
an external, continuously-gradable signal that is valid even where the base is lost, a program checked
against the train pairs being the cleanest, rather than another pass over the model's own grid outputs.

We built that arm and measured it. A 1.5B model trained to emit programs in an executable DSL, sampled and
kept only when a program reproduces every train pair, solved zero of the 101 ARC-2 evaluation tasks our
transductive system misses. It generalized weakly on the easier ARC-1, about one held-out task in nine, but
on ARC-2 the DSL cannot express the new compositional concepts, and the tasks the base misses are also the
largest grids where the small proposer exhausts the card before it can search. The external-signal lever is
therefore real but bounded by model scale on one GPU, the same wall the refinement family hits from the
other side. The lever exists but needs a larger model to grip, which is exactly the move the full contest
hardware allows (Section~\ref{sec:scale}).

One experiment on this lever is built and unrun, and its motivation changed underneath it in a way worth
recording. The community port carries a symbolic arm that we deleted wholesale (Section~\ref{sec:phantom}),
and it returns two independent things. One is a solved flag that preempts the learned search entirely,
and that half is measured harmful, taking 12 of 39 slice tasks with nothing correct to show. The other is
an object count read off the demonstration pairs on the CPU while the adapter trains, which never
preempts anything and only re-ranks beams the search has already produced. Our stub killed the second
silently along with the first, and it is a weak but free instance of exactly what this section argues for:
an external signal, valid where the base is lost, obtained by reading the demonstrations rather than the
model's own beliefs, and therefore outside the ceiling of Appendix~\ref{app:math} that binds every
selector built from model likelihoods. It costs no GPU, which is the constraint that defeated the 1.5B
program synthesiser above.

We had staged that experiment to close a three-point deficit to a public notebook. The control in
Section~\ref{sec:phantom} then showed the deficit was never there, which removes the reason we were
running it and leaves the reason we should have been running it all along. It is now a single-factor test
on top of our best configuration rather than a catch-up move, and it is the only one of the three staged
builds still worth a submission slot: the other two revert our changes toward a configuration the control
has already measured at 28.06. The prediction registered before it runs is a gain of no more than two or
three task credits, since the count is a coarse statistic that is silent on most tasks, and we note in
advance that a coarse external signal producing a small real gain and a coarse external signal producing
nothing are separated by less than the width of our error bar on a single submission.

Finally, the significance of the TTT lift is directional
rather than conclusive (Appendix~\ref{app:results}). A full-eval paired run is the obvious next
measurement.

\subsection{Scaling to the full contest hardware}
\label{sec:scale}
Everything above holds a single 16~GB card fixed, but the offline contest gives four L4 GPUs, 96~GB
together, and that hardware is where the real climb happens. As of late July 2026 the live public
leaderboard is led by one entry near 65.8\%, with second place near 38.6\% and a chasing pack in the mid
thirties, and every one of those runs uses the same offline no-internet sandbox our system does. On the
available evidence the leader is the same NVIDIA Kaggle Grandmasters lineage that won the 2025 offline
track and whose grid model our system reproduces, and their stated recipe is to move all complex reasoning
offline into a large synthetic-data pipeline and train a small model that runs fast at evaluation~\cite{arc2025}.
That framing matters for where the twenty-seven-point gap to second place actually lives. The contest's
own 2025 technical report attributes the distance between the offline field and the higher application-track
numbers to knowledge coverage and verifier-guided optimization rather than to any fundamental scientific
breakthrough~\cite{arc2025}, which is to say the remaining lever is training-data scale and a working
refinement or verification loop, not a decoding or selection trick of the kind we exhausted here. The
higher numbers often quoted for ARC-AGI-2, a 54\% Gemini refinement harness and frontier scores above 70\%,
come from a separate public track that allows internet and external compute, so they are not the bar we
measure against~\cite{arc2025}. The offline bar is the leaderboard above. The field runs test-time
adaptation, heavy synthetic-data pretraining, and program search on the full 96~GB, and our best single-card
score of 18.19\% sits below it. Our faithful reproduction of the winning lineage's own pipeline, run on the
four-L4 node it was built for, reached 30.97\% and still sits far below the leader, which locates the gap
in the base model and its training data rather than in the inference-time machinery we can run. None of our negative
results argues against that climb, but none of them tests it either, so we state the scaling case as a
falsifiable hypothesis for future work rather than as a claim.
Every wall we mapped belongs to a 4B model on a single card, and the sharpest is bimodality. The model
solves a task robustly or misses it entirely, which is the exact reason refinement and verifier-guided
selection found nothing to grip. The hypothesis is that bimodality is a property of this model's scale
and training rather than of the task. The falsifiable prediction is that a more capable base run in the
96~GB produces partially-correct candidates on tasks it does not solve outright, a measurable non-empty
near-miss band where ours is 1.5\%, and that this in turn reactivates the refinement and program-search
levers that stalled here. Three of our measurements bound the hypothesis from below. A second small
transductive model buys almost nothing, since our fine-tuned variant solves only two tasks the stock 4B
misses. A union of our own two pipelines buys as little. The deep-TTT greedy vote and the restored DFS
decoder share the same 4B and produce nested solved sets, the greedy arm adding exactly one task the
decoder missed over the tasks both attempt, and that task already sat in the decoder's own candidate pool
and was merely lost at selection, so the apparent diversity is a selection artifact rather than new
capability. The Tiny Recursive Model family, the strongest small grid models available, scores far below
our pipeline while needing more compute than one card gives it. That gap has not closed with time. The
original TRM reports 8\% on ARC-AGI-2, its 2026 denoising-recursion successor reaches only about
9.6\%~\cite{arc2025}, and the winning team's own ensemble measured a TRM arm as adding nothing to a strong
4B, so any diversity that pays must come from capability rather than from a second small vote. One published result warns against reading the
hypothesis as a bare parameter-count claim. A $\sim$200M encoder-decoder system with test-time training
and white-box filtering reports 27\% on the ARC-AGI-2 semi-private set, ahead of the 4B-based 2025
winner~\cite{longt5arc}, so method quality can dominate size, and our own four failed elaborations of
the 4B's candidate machinery (Table~\ref{tab:versions}) sharpen the same point from below. We read the
16~GB result as the place where this small-model recipe runs out. The prediction above is the test of
what replaces it, and we leave it as stated, a hypothesis with a measurement that would falsify it,
rather than as a promise.

\subsection{A routed dual-arm system}
\label{sec:dualarm}
Section~\ref{sec:limits} argued that the next lever must supply an external, continuously-gradable signal
that is valid where the base is lost, and Section~\ref{sec:scale} offered one escape, a larger
non-bimodal base, as a falsifiable hypothesis. We are building a second escape that stays on the
single-card efficiency budget rather than betting on scale, and it follows directly from two measurements
already in this paper. The AIRV vote agreement predicts correctness (Figure~\ref{fig:votes}), so the grid
model reports for free which tasks it has solved and which it has no grip on. And the base is bimodal, so
on a missed task there is nothing to refine, only something to replace.

The system routes on the first fact and fills the gap with a method that does not share the base's blind
spot. High-consensus tasks are banked with the grid model's own answer, which is most of the current
score at a fraction of the compute. Low-consensus tasks, where the transductive model scores near zero,
are routed to an induction arm, a code model that writes plain-Python grid transforms which we execute
against the train pairs. A program that reproduces every train pair is verified on observable data in a
way no vote can be, so its output becomes the first attempt and the grid model's guess falls back to the
second. The two arms are complementary by construction rather than by hope, induction expresses the
precise compositional rules transduction misses.

The design is deliberately do-no-harm. A banked task is byte-identical to the transductive submission,
and a program is trusted only when it reproduces every train pair, so the arm can only add coverage on
tasks the base was already missing. This is the property the five candidate-side levers lacked. Each of
them rewrote the vote on every task including the ones already solved, and each regressed
(Table~\ref{tab:versions}). The one loss channel, a program that fits every train pair yet is wrong on
the test and displaces a correct second attempt, is measured on the local eval before any submission. The
arm also inverts the engine economy of Section~\ref{sec:engine}. Verifying a program by running it is
cheap, and where several programs survive we rank their outputs under the grid model's per-view
likelihood, which is prefill-priced and about thirty times cheaper than the decode that produced them, so
the system generates less and checks more. The whole arm runs inside the idle two-thirds of the offline
budget the transductive submission leaves unspent.

This is the same idea whose cheap form failed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}, and it is built to answer why.
That probe used a 1.5B model emitting a fixed ARC-1 domain-specific language and solved none of the 101
missed tasks. The arm here uses a 14B code model emitting unrestricted Python, which addresses both named
causes at once, model capacity and an expressive target language.

\textbf{Status.} The first probe is a disjoint-coverage run on the single card. A 14B code model
(Qwen2.5-Coder) samples sixteen programs per task at temperature 0.8, on exactly the 101 evaluation tasks
the transductive system misses, and a program is kept only if it reproduces every train pair. It solved
none of them. More telling than the zero, across roughly 1600 programs only one task saw a program
reproduce even a single train pair, so the model is not close anywhere. The programs are not the failure
mode the arc-dsl proposer was. They are fluent and sensible. On a task whose output size changes with
every example the model wrote a clean routine that extracts and sorts rows, which crashed because its
fixed-shape hypothesis cannot produce a variable-size output, and on a task with a narrower output it
wrote correct code for the wrong rule and returned the input width. The 1.5B DSL proposer failed because
it could not express the concepts. This larger model expresses them freely and still cannot infer the
rule from a few examples. The bottleneck is inference, not expression, and a general coder does not close
it zero-shot on one card. Priming the coder with three verified worked-example solvers, one of them a
variable-size crop aimed squarely at that failure, did not change the outcome. It solved none of the 101
and lifted the partial signal only from one task to two, with nothing reaching a near-miss. Two forms of
a strong single-card coder, cold and primed, both come back empty, which is itself the result. The
bottleneck is rule inference on the hardest tasks, and neither sampling more programs nor showing worked
examples closes it at 14B on one card. A same-size reasoning model, which works
through the transformation at length before it writes code, does not change the verdict either. On a
bounded run over twelve of the missed tasks it solved none and reproduced no train pair, at roughly four
minutes of reasoning per sample. Three model classes, a small restricted proposer, a strong instruct
coder cold and primed, and a reasoning model, all fail to induct these hardest tasks on a single card,
and the near-miss band stays empty throughout. We read this as a decisive single-card result rather than
a tuning gap. The router is sound, vote agreement does report which tasks the grid model has solved, but
the arm it would route to cannot grip the tasks the grid model misses without more compute than one card
provides. That is the same wall Section~\ref{sec:scale} predicts, now measured from the induction side,
and it is where the second arm's value waits on the full contest hardware.

\subsection{A stronger public decoder, and its coupling to training}
\label{sec:architects}
The clearest evidence that the gap in Section~\ref{sec:results} is method rather than model arrived from
outside the project. A public notebook ports the 2024 ARC-Prize-winning pipeline, the ARChitects', onto our
exact base and reports a score in the low thirties on ARC-AGI-2, more than double our operating point on the
same weights. The port's lineage deserves naming. The pipeline is published by Ivan Sorokin, the author of
the NVARC base model itself, as the notebook \texttt{arc2\_qwen3\_unsloth\_flash\_lora\_batch4\_queue}, and
the variant we study is its community re-target to the 2026 contest. Everything we report about this system
is reproduction and measurement of that public work, credited to its authors, and our contribution is
confined to the analysis around it. It bundles two changes against our recipe. The per-task training is an order of magnitude
heavier, a rank-256 adapter that also trains the embeddings and the output head at an eight-thousand-token
context, where ours is rank sixteen at two thousand. And the decoder is not our greedy vote. In its place it
runs a depth-first search over every completion whose running probability stays above a fixed floor, then
selects among the survivors by the product of their likelihoods across fresh augmentations rather than by a
majority.

We had named this decoder as the untried lever ourselves and never built it, so the first question is whether
it is a separable part we could capture on one 16~GB card without the heavy training it ships with. We test it
directly. Holding the adapter fixed at our rig-feasible configuration, we decode each task twice on the
identical trained model, once with our greedy vote and once with the search-and-select, and score both against
the solution. Only the decoder moves.

On our light adapter the search loses, and it loses for a reason that names the coupling. Over eighteen of the
trainable tasks the greedy vote scored the equivalent of 5.17 solved tasks to the search's 3.33, 28.7 percent
against 18.5. The mechanism is visible in the candidate counts. On task after task the search returns nothing
at all, because no whole-grid completion clears the probability floor. A grid runs to about nine hundred
tokens, and a little uncertainty at each cell compounds across all of them, so an adapter that is not sharply
peaked pushes the probability of even its best completion under the floor. That floor is calibrated for a model
trained hard enough to stay almost certain across the whole grid. Ours is not, so the search starves and
forfeits tasks the greedy path still lands. The decoder is not a model-agnostic component we can lift. It is
tuned to the sharp adapter the heavy training produces, and the two are one system rather than two levers. This
is also why we could never have reached the field's level by swapping decoders on the pipeline we already had.

\textbf{Status.} Resolved, and the coupling did not save the decoder. Over thirty trainable tasks the rank-128
adapter, with the embeddings and output head trained as far toward their configuration as a 16~GB card holds,
gives the greedy vote \textbf{24.4\%} against the search's \textbf{13.3\%}. The search fell further behind than
it was at rank 32, not closer, because the sharper adapter still starves the probability floor on most tasks
while the heavier training only helped the vote. On every configuration a 16~GB card can run, rank 32 and
rank 128, the search loses to the vote. We read this as the decoder being inseparable from the full rank-256,
eight-thousand-token training it ships with, which one card can hold but cannot serve inside the contest
budget (Section~\ref{sec:results}), so the reach to the field's low thirties stays a claim to be settled on
the contest hardware rather than a lever we could capture here. The
lift we did capture, from 13.61 to 18.19, came from the training depth alone. A first hidden-set submission
of the reproduction itself, run faithfully with the community port's bolted-on symbolic arm still active,
scored 16.81\%. Two facts inside that number matter more than its size. The public-to-hidden transfer is
flat, 16.2\% on the evaluation slice against 16.81\% hidden, where every candidate-side lever we built
ourselves lost points crossing that gap. And the symbolic arm is measured poison, on the evaluation slice
it preempted twelve of thirty-nine tasks with its own answer and got none of them right, so the faithful
reproduction understates the pipeline. The arm is stripped and the corrected variant is measured next. That measurement is in, and it also surfaced
a second inherited defect, the community port had split the decoder's four-view batches into pairs, halving
search throughput. With the symbolic arm removed and the original batching restored, the same evaluation
slice rises from 16.2\% to \textbf{27.2\%}, no tasks preempted, no timeouts, and half again more correct
answers present in the search beams. Eleven points of the gap between the reproduction and its source's
claim were the port's own mutations. Submitted to the hidden set, the restored variant scored
\textbf{30.56\%}, against 16.81\% for the same pipeline as ported and 18.19\% for our own best
single-card configuration. Three things follow. The transfer property held a third time, 27.2\% on the
evaluation slice against 30.56\% hidden, so this pipeline carries its public number across the gap that
every candidate-side lever of ours fell through. The original author's claimed tier is credible, since
a faithful restoration lands within striking distance of it while the ported form did not. And the
result is a reproduction, not a contribution of ours. What this work adds around it is the measurement,
the isolation of the two port defects, the paired evaluation that priced each one, and the placement of
the restored number against a single-card baseline so the hardware gap becomes visible.

\subsection{The selection leak, measured on the candidate pool}
\label{sec:selection}
A search-and-select decoder is only as good as its selector, and the restored pipeline of
Section~\ref{sec:architects} throws away correct answers it has already found. To measure how many, we ran a
build identical to the submitted one except for a gated dump of the entire candidate pool. It records, for
every test output, each distinct candidate grid together with the number of augmented views that produced
it, the beam likelihood of each view, and the per-augmentation likelihoods the selector consumes. Over forty
evaluation tasks the run produced 60 test outputs across 39 tasks, one of the forty yielding no decoded

The exercise also carries a warning about the slice it ran on. An earlier reading of these same forty tasks
found three discordant outputs favouring the confidence-weighted rule over the published one, with a clean
mechanism behind it, so we shipped the switch, and a 120-task run then reversed it. This sweep points the
opposite way on the same tasks, that discarding the vote count beats keeping it, on the evidence of one
output in sixty and as the best of sixteen rules tried. Two contradictory prescriptions of equal statistical
weight, from one evaluation slice. We therefore record the count-free rule as measured and leave the
deployed selector alone.

What we carry forward is the negative, and it is sharper than the headroom number by itself. The selection
leak is worth roughly six points on this slice, and it is not recoverable by rescoring what the pipeline
already computes, because every statistic in the pool ranks the discarded correct grid below the chosen one.
Closing it needs a signal the pool does not contain, consistency against the task's own train pairs, which
is the verifier direction Section~\ref{sec:limits} finds silent exactly where it is needed. The contribution
of this measurement is to eliminate the cheaper alternative. Reweighting is exhausted, and the instrument
for any future attempt is the pool dump itself, which makes selection rules testable offline without
further contest compute.

The signal that paragraph asked for has since been built, and it fails too. It is worth reporting because
the construction avoids the objection that sank our earlier verifier, which scored an adapter rather than a
candidate and so could not choose between two candidates the same adapter produced. Here a candidate is
scored as evidence. An output proposed for the test input is either a true instance of the task's rule or
it is not, so appending the pair of test input and candidate to the task's demonstrations either hands the
model a fourth true example or hands it a contradiction. We measure the negative log-likelihood of a
held-out real demonstration's output twice, once from the remaining demonstrations alone and once with the
candidate injected, and average the difference across held-out choices. A negative delta means the
candidate behaved like a true example of the rule. Scoring a difference rather than a likelihood cancels
per-task and per-fold difficulty, and the whole thing costs forward passes rather than decodes, so it is
affordable over every candidate inside the contest budget.

A cheap gate ran first, over all 172 evaluation test outputs, scoring the true grid against four
distractors, the test input unchanged, the true grid with two colours swapped, the true grid transposed,
and another task's answer. The true grid ranked first on 133 of them against a chance rate of one in five,
which read alone looks like a working signal. The margins say otherwise. The median winning margin is
0.0043 nats and the median losing margin is 0.0031, so the two distributions sit on top of each other, and
58 percent of the wins are by less than 0.005. The shape of the failures matters more than the rate.
Another task's answer loses 171 times out of 172, so the score reliably rejects a grid unrelated to the
rule, while transpose beats the truth 17 times, colour swap 11 and the unchanged input 10. Those three
preserve the structure of the answer and change its content, which is precisely what a decoder's
near-misses are.

Against the real pool it is worse than the rule it was meant to improve (Table~\ref{tab:roundtrip}).
Ranking by the round trip alone solves 7 of the 60 outputs where the deployed rule solves 15, losing eight

than correctness.

That something is grid complexity. A simple, repetitive, low-entropy candidate is cheap to append and
perturbs the model very little, so its delta sits near zero, while a complex correct answer perturbs more.
The score therefore rewards candidates that are easy to encode over candidates that are right, which is
also why the unchanged test input, usually the structurally cheapest grid on offer, beat the truth ten
times in the gate.

One qualification is owed. The scoring ran against the unadapted base model, because the per-task adapters
that produced this pool were discarded with the contest container, and a sharper adapted model would react
more strongly to a contradictory demonstration. We report the number as a floor for that reason. It is not
a floor we expect to lift far, because the failure is systematic rather than weak, and a complexity bias
does not dissolve under better adaptation.

Two further attacks followed, on the attempt slots and on the grids themselves, and both close the same
way. The first observed that our two attempts come off a single ranking, so when the top two candidates are
near-duplicates the second slot restates the first, and tested spending attempt two on the highest-ranked
candidate at least $k$ cells away from attempt one. At every $k$ tried the rule loses one output and gains
none, because the geometry runs opposite to its premise. On \texttt{4c3d4a41\_1} the correct grid is rank
two, one cell from rank one, so any diversity requirement skips it, and on \texttt{3dc255db\_0} the correct
grid ranks sixth while sitting two cells from the chosen wrong answer. When this pipeline misses, its miss
is a near-duplicate of the truth, and the distant candidates a diversity rule promotes are noise.

That anatomy suggested the opposite mechanism, fusing the near-duplicates instead of avoiding them. A
per-cell weighted vote across same-shape candidates can emit a grid that appears in no candidate, which
grid-level voting cannot do by construction. It emits nineteen novel grids on this pool and not one of them
is correct. Count-weighted fusion in the second attempt slot is exactly neutral, and every other weighting
regresses. The failure case is the informative part. On \texttt{3dc255db\_0} thirteen same-shape candidates
fuse to a grid two cells from the truth, and they are the same two cells the selected answer gets wrong.
The candidates' errors are correlated, so no vote among them can cancel what they all agree on.

We stop here, and the stopping is the finding. The leak is the largest measured gap in this system, 6.4
points on this slice and roughly fifteen task credits at contest scale, and it has now turned back four
attacks with unrelated mechanisms, sixteen reweightings of the pool's own statistics, an out-of-pool
consistency score that ranks the answers backwards, a diversity-forced second attempt, and cell-level
fusion. The correlated errors explain all four at once. The model does not hold a noisy version of the
right rule that clever aggregation could denoise, it holds the wrong rule, and every candidate it produces
expresses that same wrong rule. This is the bimodality of Section~\ref{sec:scale} surfacing at cell level,
and it relocates the leak from a selection problem to a training problem. The information needed to pick
the correct grid is absent from the pool and from the model that produced it, so any repair has to change
what the model believes, not how its outputs are ranked. Appendix~\ref{app:math} states this as a
dominance proposition over the pool's statistics and verifies its premise on all four missed outputs.

\subsection{Revisiting the big-grid wall with the host's memory}
\label{sec:biggrid}
The journey of one cap tells this subsection's story. Thirty-six of the 120 evaluation tasks have a train
pair larger than a thousand tokens, and their per-task training runs out of memory on 16~GB, so from the
first TTT submission onward they were excluded and served without adaptation. We tested that exclusion once,
early, by forcing TTT onto them with the training context capped at 600 tokens, the most that then fit. It
scored 10.6\% against 12.8\% without, and we recorded the loss as ``the VRAM exclusion is a genuine ceiling,
not a missed opportunity.'' That sentence survived every draft since. Rereading the measurement now, it does
not support the sentence. A single train pair on these tasks is at least 1010 tokens, so a 600-token cap
admits exactly one demonstration into the training context, and the adapter learned each step from a single
example where the small tasks see several. What we measured was truncation, not the ceiling.

The second look came from turning away from the card. The rig carries 64~GB of system memory that sits idle
while the 16~GB of VRAM starves, and during TTT the base model is frozen, only the small adapter trains, so
the 7.5~GB of frozen weights never need to live on the GPU at all. Streaming them from host memory through
a device map, with a 6~GiB resident-weight budget on the card, frees the VRAM for what actually grows with
grid size, the activations, and those stay small here because the 16-token vocabulary keeps the output layer
tiny even at long sequence lengths. Under that arrangement the training context rises from the old 600-token
cap to 8000 tokens, above the 7471-token worst case across all thirty-six tasks, which is to say literally
full context, the same leave-one-out signal the small tasks have always had. The cost is streaming time, the
frozen weights cross the PCIe bus every step, which is unaffordable inside a contest budget and nearly free
on the rig.

\textbf{Status.} The staged probe has run its first two gates and the ceiling sentence is already refuted
in its strong form. The smoke stage trained the single largest excluded task end to end, 14.6~GB peak on
the 16~GB card with the base streaming from host memory, every mechanical check passing. The six-task
stratified panel then produced the first solve this excluded set has ever yielded, task 8e5c0c38 moves from
zero of two test outputs without adaptation to one of two with its full-context adapter, and no panel task
got worse. So at least one of the thirty-six is winnable, and what was recorded as a genuine ceiling was,
at least in part, the truncation artifact Section~\ref{sec:ablations} now flags. Training losses tell the
mechanism, adapters on these tasks now converge to near zero where the 600-token cap never could. One
operational wrinkle surfaced, on the longest-context tasks Windows silently overcommits GPU memory into
host RAM rather than failing, which crawls training, and the affected adapters were retracted and retrained
at a lower resident-weight budget. The full thirty-six run is complete and the count is decisive. Across the
excluded set's 47 test outputs, the unadapted system solves 4 in this paired harness and the full-context
adapters solve \textbf{8}, five tasks flipping to solved against one flipping away, that one a displacement
the two-attempt format removes in deployment by carrying both arms. Four of the largest tasks remain
honestly flagged undertrained where even the reduced-budget training exceeded its time cap, so the 8 is a
floor with headroom. The doubled count settles the question this subsection opened. What we recorded as a
genuine ceiling was in substantial part a truncation artifact, and the excluded third of the evaluation is
partially winnable on this card with the host's memory carrying the frozen weights.

One postscript, because it may make the streaming unnecessary. The activation growth that forced the frozen
weights off the card in the first place was measured under an attention kernel the framework had chosen by
default, and forcing the cuDNN backend instead trains rank 256 at eight thousand tokens fully resident in
14.86~GB (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Our big-grid configuration is rank 32, well below that, so these
tasks would plausibly train resident and pay no streaming cost at all, which would move the arrangement
from unaffordable inside a contest budget to ordinary. We have not measured that cell, so we state it as
the obvious next probe rather than as a result, and we leave the streaming numbers above as they were
measured.

First, the search is not time-starved, it is capability-starved. Sixty-five percent of tasks never

A later experiment supplies the signal the second reading says is missing, and it arrived from an
experiment aimed at something else. Asking whether a task's demonstrations underdetermine its rule,
we trained leave-one-demonstration-out adapters per task and compared their candidate pools. As a
repair the answer is a flat null: over 20 outputs the union of every subset adapter equals the full
adapter exactly, so removing a demonstration does not move the adapter to a different reachable
hypothesis and the 65\% stays a property of the base model. But the \emph{disagreement} between
those subset pools separates the outcomes that training loss cannot. Mean pairwise pool Jaccard is
\textbf{0.373} on outputs where the full adapter found the truth and \textbf{0.015} where it did
not, against a same-configuration replicate baseline of 0.543. Small samples, seven and four
outputs, so we report it as a lead rather than a policy. The direction is what matters: when a task's
rule is determined, adapters trained on different subsets of its demonstrations agree about what to
propose, and when it is not, they share almost nothing. That is a per-task confidence measure
computed before the answer is known, which is precisely what a budget allocator needs and what the
loss-cut rule lacked. Building the policy on it is future work, and its cost, one extra adapter per
task, is the same reclaimed budget the cap frees.

The visiting-order question received the same treatment before any engineering was spent on it.
Best-first enumeration, the anytime alternative to threshold-DFS that the general decoding
literature offers and that no published ARC system uses, makes a testable claim: under truncation,
expanding nodes in cumulative-likelihood order should reach the first correct grid in far fewer
expansions than the depth-first order actually taken. We instrumented the deployed decoder to log
every node expansion and every emission over sixteen stratified tasks, 30{,}480 batched forward
passes and 217 completed candidates, twelve of the sixteen decodes budget-truncated, and replayed
each recorded tree under both orders at equal information. Ten tasks put a correct grid in the tree
and are the ones that can discriminate. Across them the median saving to the first truth is
\textbf{2.9 percent} and no task is worse under best-first, but the distribution is the finding
rather than the median: three tasks save nothing at all, four save between one and three percent,
and the entire effect sits in three tasks saving 13, 26 and 60 percent.

The mechanism is visible in the logs and explains the shape. Nearly all negative log-likelihood
accrues at a handful of branch points, the forced runs between them are order-invariant, and
threshold-DFS sorts each node's children before descending, so it already traverses in nearly
likelihood order. Best-first can only recover the cases where the tree's first subtree is both deep
and wrong, which is a minority of tasks. A lever that pays on three tasks in sixteen, all of them
already truncating, does not justify replacing the decoder.

We record this as a refutation of our own prediction rather than as a revision. An earlier draft of
this section argued that anytime enumeration was the strongest unexplored lever available and that
the published absence of best-first search for ARC was an opportunity. The measurement cost one
logging run and says otherwise.

A cap at 1200 seconds therefore costs zero measured solves and frees about 7800 seconds per worker
against a 42480-second wall, roughly eighteen percent. Decide-early, heavy-tailed, and with no usable
progress signal is precisely the regime in which restart theory says a hard cutoff plus a fresh attempt
dominates waiting, and Appendix~\ref{app:math} already derives the fixed-cutoff objective for exactly
this queue. We derived the mathematics and never applied the policy, which is the same failure this
paper records elsewhere in a different costume.

\textbf{We then applied it, and the timing model did not survive contact with the hidden set.} The
cap had never once been tested cleanly: v7.3 declared 1200 and ran 1800 because the build script's
replacement targeted a form of the guard that exists only inside a commented-out cell, and v7.4
really ran 1200 but also fired 24 unbudgeted greedy fills, so two factors moved and its 29.44 is
void rather than adverse. The clean test changes one live literal in the replicate notebook, the
artifact whose code cells hash identical to the runs that scored 30.56 and 32.64, with the config
banner's default moved to match so it cannot again print a cap the code does not run. It scored
\textbf{29.31}.

That is 1.33 points below the same-configuration mean, 1.13 standard deviations, and below all but
one member of the eight-run family. By this paper's own rule a difference of under two points is not a
result, and we hold to the rule: the cap is \emph{not} shown to be harmful. What it is not is
supported. The prediction, recorded before the run and derived from our own timing log, was neutral
to positive on the reasoning that the four tasks past 1200 seconds were all misses consuming 26.3\%
of the budget while the slowest solve arrived at 1118 seconds. The observation is the lowest score
any variant of this configuration has produced. A draw this low or lower has probability 0.05 under
the measured family, so it is weak evidence against the prediction and no evidence for it.

The instructive part is why a well-grounded timing argument can fail. Our timing log was collected
on one card at a per-task granularity, and it says nothing about how a 240-task queue interacts with
four workers and a wall. Capping does not only remove doomed tasks, it changes which tasks land on
which worker and where each worker's tail falls, and the same session-speed sensitivity that gives
this pipeline a one-point standard deviation acts on that rearrangement too. A single submission
cannot separate those. Settling it needs the paired design this section spent its length arguing
for, which at one submission per day and $\sigma \approx 1$ is roughly eight runs, and we record the
cost rather than paying it.

The proposal is to cap hard and spend the reclaimed time on a second independent adapter for tasks that
missed, unioning the candidate pools. Seven thousand eight hundred seconds per worker buys roughly
fifteen restarts each, sixty across the node, against about one hundred and fifty misses on a 240-task
set. Whether that is worth anything depends entirely on one quantity we do not have: whether a second
independent run fails the same tasks.

Here the pipeline turns out to forbid its own experiment. Every stochastic choice in the deployed solver
is seeded by a constant: the adapter initialiser at 42, the trainer at 42, the training augmentation
draw at 1, and the evaluation augmentation draw at 2. Rerunning a task therefore reproduces the same
adapter bit for bit, and a restart in the naive sense returns the identical pool. The lever exists only
if the training augmentation seed is varied, which changes which sixteen of the dihedral-and-colour
permutation space the adapter ever sees, and that is a genuinely different training set for the same
task. A determinism that reads as good hygiene is, for this purpose, the thing standing in the way.

We built the probe that answers it, and three details of its design are load-bearing. It trains the
deployed configuration on the same task under different augmentation seeds and records, per test
output, whether the truth is present in the decoded pool, so the comparison is union against single.
Tasks are sampled stratified by their Kaggle outcome rather than taken in order, because of the first
twenty tasks only four ever had the truth in the pool and fifteen are outright misses that cannot
discriminate between seeds while still inflating the denominator; rates are reweighted back to the
slice. The scope of any answer is narrower than the question sounds: the augmentation always applies
all eight dihedral views and the seed selects only the colour permutation and example order, and we
measured the resulting training sets to be almost disjoint as text, so a null licenses ``colour
permutation restarts do not help'' and not ``restarts do not help''.

The third detail is a negative control, and it is not optional. The search admits a continuation on a
hard threshold over half-precision logits, so a rounding difference at a borderline token prunes or
admits an entire subtree and two runs of the \emph{same} seed need not agree. We measured that noise
directly: against a cache-free reference the decoder's scores agree only to about $2\times10^{-3}$,
which is the same order as the 0.0043-nat margin scale Section~\ref{sec:selection} measures for the
selector itself. Without subtracting a same-seed replicate, ``union exceeds single'' would look
positive whether or not seeds decorrelate, so the reported quantity is cross-seed disagreement minus
replicate disagreement.

The answer is no, and the negative control is what makes that statement worth anything. Over 65 cells
covering 19 tasks and 30 test outputs, the three seeds place the truth in the pool for 11, 17 and 15
outputs, and their union is 17. The union equals the best single seed exactly: seed two alone finds
everything all three find together, and this held at every sample size we inspected as the run
proceeded. Reweighted to the slice that is a 4.4-point apparent gain over the mean single seed, and
reporting that number would have been the error the control exists to prevent. Rerunning the
\emph{same} seed disagrees with itself on 12.5\% of outputs; different seeds disagree on 13.3\%. The
net is $+0.008$, which is indistinguishable from zero, so what the naive contrast measures is the
decoder's own numerical nondeterminism and not any property of the training draw.

Two things make the null more credible than a null usually is. The bias in this design runs toward the
hypothesis rather than away: 48 of 65 decodes were cut at their budget, so the probe is uniformly
weaker than deployment, and decorrelation generically rises as success probability falls toward the
noise floor. We failed to find an effect under conditions that would exaggerate one. And the sample was
stratified precisely so the cells would land where seeds could differ, yet every hit lies in the
reachable stratum, 17 of 21 outputs, with nothing at all from the nine outputs drawn from tasks the
deployed run missed. The tasks a restart would need to rescue are not close to being rescued.

What this licenses is narrower than it sounds and we hold to the narrower claim. The augmentation
always applies all eight dihedral views, so the seed re-rolls the colour permutation and the example
order, and the finding is that re-rolling those does not change which tasks the adapter can reach. It
does not establish that no restart of any kind helps. What it does establish is that the cheap version,
the one the reclaimed cap could actually have paid for, does not, so the scheduling lever of this
section is closed and the 65 percent of tasks that never produce the truth remain a property of the
model rather than of the budget. Fidelity supports reading it that way: across the 19 shared tasks the
probe's mean training loss tracks the deployed run's at a median ratio of 0.93, and the two agree on
outcome for 15 of 19.

Building the probe also produced a result about our own instrument that we report because it nearly cost
us the experiment. On the reproduction stack the model's tokenizer cannot represent the prompt format the
model was trained on. It is a word-level vocabulary of sixteen tokens with no merges, and the entries
for the two turn markers are unreachable: encoding the word for either returns nothing, and the user
and assistant preambles encode identically. The completion-only loss mask keys on exactly those two
ids to find the assistant spans, so every label was masked, the cross-entropy ran over zero targets and
returned NaN, and the gradient norm was zero at every rank and optimizer we tried. Nothing raised. Left
undetected it would have trained every adapter to NaN, decoded an empty pool for every task, and
reported that seeds do not decorrelate, which is both the answer that ends the investigation and the
answer we had no independent reason to doubt. The deployed runs do not have this defect, since the
measurement run's recorded losses are finite and converged, which is what pins the intended
tokenization and let us reconstruct it exactly.

Porting the deployed solver to a current software stack surfaced a portability result worth more than
the probe, and we are careful below to say exactly how far it is established. The depth-first search
recurses with a single key-value cache object and calls the model repeatedly inside a loop, passing that
same cache each time. That is correct only if a forward pass leaves its input cache untouched, which was
true of the legacy tuple interface the pipeline was written against, where the returned cache was a new
object. Current library versions replaced it with cache objects that append in place. On those versions
the second branch of every search node would continue from an already-extended cache, so positions
advance past what the cache holds and the search explores from the wrong prefix. Restoring the original
behaviour needs only a truncation back to the branch point before each call, after which the algorithm
is unchanged, and that is what our port does.

The repair is now verified, though not in the way the first draft of this section claimed. We tested it
by enumerating the search tree with the pruning threshold set so loose that nothing is ever discarded,
under which the cached decoder and a cache-free reference that recomputes from scratch must agree
exactly. They do: identical candidate sets, with scores matching to the numerical tolerance quoted
above. The truncation also agrees with an independent implementation that slices the cache tensors
directly and never calls the library's own truncation method. What we have \emph{not} observed is the
unpatched decoder failing on the deployed stack, since that stack pins the older interface, so the
consequence of the dependency remains an inference from the interface change rather than a reproduction
of the fault. We are explicit about that split because the alternative is the mistake of
Section~\ref{sec:phantom}, where a plausible mechanism was fitted to an effect nobody had confirmed.
What is solid is the dependency itself: the pipeline every entry in the
thirty-point cluster is running relies on a deprecated interface contract, that reliance is invisible in
the notebook, it produces no warning, and it is held in place only by the competition image's pinned
versions. If that image moves, the failure is not a crash in the general case but a search proceeding
from a corrupted prefix, which would surface as a quiet drop in solve rate that no log would explain.
A dependency tree really can be worth points, which Section~\ref{sec:phantom} claimed on bad evidence
and withdrew. This is what a defensible version looks like, and it is offered as a specific mechanism
with a stated failure mode, still awaiting its own control.

\subsection{Perceptual pre-fitting on the test input}
\label{sec:prefit}
Every result above spends its adapter budget on the demonstration pairs. The test input grid is
never a loss target, only a decode-time prompt, which means the largest single piece of
task-specific information we hold is discarded before the first gradient step. Section~\ref{sec:related}
records that this is inherited from the ARChitects rather than chosen by us. Three published results
suggest it might be expensive. CompressARC~\cite{compressarc} reaches roughly 20\% on ARC-AGI-1
evaluation by gradient-fitting a 76K-parameter network per task to compress that task's own grids,
the test input included and only the answer withheld, with no pretraining at all, which is a floor
on how much signal per-task grid structure carries by itself. The LongT5 system~\cite{longt5arc}
gains 2.08 points from UL2 denoising of grid tokens, though offline and on demonstration grids.
And the self-supervised test-time-training line~\cite{sun2020,ttmae} is built entirely on adapting
to the unlabeled test instance.

So we ran the composition: a masked-cell denoising objective on this task's test input, expressed
inside the deployed prompt format so the existing collator supervises it unmodified, for a fixed
number of steps before the ordinary demonstration objective. Corrupted grid in the user turn, clean
grid in the assistant turn, cells dropped at 0.25 to a colour drawn from the task's own palette
complement so the model is never asked to invent a colour the task does not use.

The third arm is the reason the experiment is worth running at all. A two-arm design cannot separate
instance-specific grid fluency from the brute fact that the treated arm took more gradient steps than
the control, and an unattributable positive is how three of this project's retracted explanations
began. So the control arm denoises \emph{a different task's} test input for the same number of steps
on the same volume of data. If pre-fitting gains and the control does not, the effect follows the
grid. If both gain, the honest finding is that more steps help and the interesting claim is dead.

We record the prior-art position before the outcome, because it does not depend on the outcome. The
precise composition appears to be unclaimed, but the fair characterisation is adjacent-negative
rather than unexplored. Akyurek et al.~\cite{akyurek2024} defined the neighbouring objective,
attributed it explicitly to the Sun et al. lineage, ablated it, and dropped it when it lost by 1.9
points; their loss targets were demonstration inputs of synthetic leave-one-out tasks rather than the
real test input, and the ablation was on BIG-Bench Hard rather than ARC, but a reviewer will reach
for it first and should. CompressARC establishes that fitting to the test input at test time works,
as an entire from-scratch solver rather than as a warm-up. The two adjacent ARC-side measurements
point opposite ways, which is the only reason the question is still live.

We stated a prediction in advance so that it could be scored: a null, because the demonstration
inputs already carry the palette, the object vocabulary and the symmetry group of the test input by
construction, so the marginal information in $x_\star$ is small, and because the perceptual account
of ARC failure that motivates the intervention is weakest exactly in our regime, the 80\% perception
figure resting on vision models reading rendered images rather than on text-grid models with literal
cell access.

\textbf{The prediction was correct, and the null is unusually clean.} Forty pre-fit steps per cell
over eight stratified tasks, 900 seconds of decode each, 24 cells: the truth is in the pool for
\textbf{7} of 14 outputs under the deployed configuration, \textbf{7} under pre-fitting on the
task's own test input, and \textbf{6} under the control that denoises a different task's grid for
the same number of steps on the same volume of data. Not one output changed status. Zero gained,
zero lost, so the intervention did not even trade coverage, which is the signature of a change the
downstream objective absorbs entirely rather than one that perturbs it.

The denoising itself worked. Each pre-fit stage supervised 870 label positions per sample across
sixteen corrupted views and ran in about 33 seconds, on grids from $10\times20$ to $30\times30$. The
adapter learned to reconstruct the instance's grid and that competence transferred nothing to the
transformation objective. Read against the prior art, this places an ARC-side companion beside
Akyurek et al.'s BIG-Bench Hard ablation of the neighbouring loss: putting a generative objective on
input grids inside test-time training was measured to lose there, and measured to do nothing here,
on the one variant they did not run, the real unlabeled test input under a masked-cell rather than
autoregressive form. CompressARC's per-task grid signal is therefore either already supplied by
pretraining or not reachable by this route on a model of this kind. We report it as a kill, and note
that the control arm is what makes it one: without the third arm, ``7 against 7'' would have been an
uninterpretable wash rather than a measurement.

Two readings follow, and the second is the one that matters.

First, the mechanism is memory and not computation. At 1398 and 1642 tokens the fourth lane costs
nothing measurable, which is the bandwidth account behaving exactly as predicted. At 1994 and 2730 the
same width costs twenty-five to thirty-two times more per step. Nothing about the arithmetic changed
between those rows; what changed is that reserved memory crossed somewhere between 12.7 and
14.2~GB on a 16~GB card, after which the platform pages to host memory rather than failing.

Second, \textbf{the majority of the evaluation set is on the wrong side of that threshold}. Over 172
test outputs the prompt length has median 2198 tokens, first quartile 1610 and third quartile 3018.
\textbf{57\% of outputs exceed the shortest prefix at which width four collapsed, and 74\% exceed the
longest at which it was still free.} So a single-card deployment of this configuration spends most of
its decode budget running one to two orders of magnitude below the speed the same hardware delivers
at half the width, and it does so on precisely the tasks with the most demonstration content.

This is the sharpest instance we have of the ceiling in the title, and it is not the one we expected
to find. The wall is usually described as capacity, whether the model and its adapter fit. Here
everything fits, the configuration runs to completion, and the cost appears entirely as throughput.
Since the search is deadline-bounded in three places, a decode running twenty-five times slower does
not merely take longer, it truncates earlier and the candidates it would have found never exist.
Throughput is coverage, and this constant costs most of it.

The remedy is a two-line policy rather than a method change: choose the batch width from the prompt
length so reserved memory stays under the cliff, which for this model means width four below roughly
1650 tokens and width two above it. It preserves the same thirty-two views and changes no score by
construction, since lanes are independent and only the grouping differs.

Three honest limits on the claim, and the third is the one that bounds it hardest.

The platform matters: this card runs Windows, where exceeding device memory pages silently to host
rather than raising, so on Linux the same configuration would be expected to fail outright rather
than degrade.

The contest node's L4s carry 24~GB each, and extrapolating our peak-memory curve puts width four's
cliff near 3200 prompt tokens there rather than 1650, which is roughly the top fifth of our prompt
distribution instead of most of it.

And the inference stack is not the same one. Our probes run plain 	exttt{transformers} with
	exttt{peft}, because the deployed path uses Unsloth and Unsloth requires Triton, which has no
native-Windows backend. The submitted notebook calls 	exttt{FastLanguageModel.for\_inference},
whose fused kernels and attention implementation are precisely what decides whether activation memory
grows linearly or quadratically in prompt length. So the cliff we measure may be a property of our
stack rather than of the pipeline, we cannot test the alternative on this hardware, and we make no
claim about the deployed runs. What we do claim is narrower and still useful: the batch width is an
undocumented constant, on a 16~GB Windows card with a stock stack it is the difference between 130
and 4100 milliseconds a step for most of the evaluation set, and anyone porting this pipeline to a
single consumer card without Unsloth inherits that silently.

\subsection{The complete ledger: what is closed, what is open, and why}
\label{sec:ledger}
This section exists because the scattered negatives above are more useful collected than distributed,
and because the most valuable thing a compute-poor entry can hand the next one is a map of where not
to spend. Everything here is measured on this pipeline; none of it is argued.

\paragraph{The headline count.} \textbf{Twenty-four levers were built and measured. None moved the
hidden score.} That is the finding, not an apology for one: a system whose remaining knobs are all
worth less than its own measurement noise is at a ceiling, and the useful thing to publish is which
knobs and why. Two of the twenty-four produced a genuine positive on their own terms without moving
coverage --- task retrieval by demonstration embedding, and warm-starting an adapter from a
same-rule neighbour --- and both sit \emph{upstream} of the candidate pool. Every lever that produced
nothing at all sits downstream of it, on candidates the search has already generated or on how time
is divided between them. We did not notice that pattern until the ledger was assembled, and it is the
single thing we would most like a reader to take from this section: the theorem bounds the
downstream half, the budget table says the upstream half is starved, and we spent most of a season
optimising the bounded one.

A second pattern is about cost rather than direction. Five of the closures required no GPU at all.
The selection bound is a proof. Sub-grid cropping was closed by measuring that 5.8\% of cells in
ARC-AGI-2 grids are removable margin. Run-length serialization was closed by observing that a
16-token vocabulary has no spare token for a run marker, and its lossless variant by measuring that
uniform row deletion truncates the true answer on 6.7\% of tasks. The tail-dropping account of our
own score variance was refuted by simulating the task queue against the wall. Each of those took
minutes and pre-empted an overnight run; several were only attempted after the expensive version had
already been planned. Pricing an idea before building it is the cheapest discipline available in a
compute-poor project and we adopted it late.

\paragraph{One number organises the map.} Sixty-five percent of tasks never produce the correct grid
under \emph{any} of the sixteen augmented views, and they consume seventy-one percent of the budget
doing it. When the truth does reach the candidate pool, the deployed selector places it in the top two
essentially always, 18 of 18 on freshly generated tasks. The system is therefore
\textbf{generation-starved, not selection-starved or time-starved}, and every closed lever below is
closed because it operates downstream of generation on a pool that either already contains the answer
or never will.

\paragraph{Seven architectural constraints, and which of them we actually probed.} The closures above
are consequences of a small number of structural facts about the system, and separating the two makes
clear which walls are load-bearing.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The base model has a sixteen-token vocabulary.} It emits grid tokens and control ids and
nothing else. Chain-of-thought, self-critique, natural-language hypotheses, verifier feedback in text
and program emission are all structurally unavailable to it, which removes most of the contemporary
reasoning literature from consideration in one stroke. This is the deepest constraint in the system
and \textbf{we never attacked it}.
\item \textbf{Gradients flow only on output grids.} Inherited from the ARChitects as an explicit design
choice. Probed: pre-fitting on the test input returned an exact null.
\item \textbf{Two hundred and forty independent adaptations that share nothing.} A fresh adapter per
task, discarded after use. Nothing learned on one task reaches another. \textbf{Unprobed.}
\item \textbf{A narrow augmentation space.} Eight dihedral transforms crossed with colour permutation
and example order. No crops, tiling, rescaling, or demonstrations synthesised from the task's own
structure. Sixteen views sample a space we defined narrowly. \textbf{Largely unprobed.}
\item \textbf{Every budget is wall-clock, not operations.} Three deadlines in seconds. This is why one
immutable artifact scores 30.56, 32.64 and 28.47, and why throughput converts into coverage.
\item \textbf{The search admits on a hard threshold over half-precision logits.} Identical runs
disagree on 12.5\% of outputs and share only 0.543 of their pools.
\item \textbf{The base is bimodal per task.} It internalises a rule or concentrates on a wrong one,
with no partial-credit middle. This is what kills refinement, verification-gated resampling and every
method that assumes a gradient of partial correctness.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The two surfaces an earlier draft called open, and what happened to them.} We named
cross-task transfer and the augmentation space as the two places with a plausible mechanism, no
measurement against them, and no published work claiming them. Both have since been measured, and
the way each closed is more informative than the closure.

\textbf{Cross-task transfer works and does not pay.} Task embeddings do recover rule identity:
mean-pooled hidden states over a task's demonstrations separate same-rule from different-rule
instances at AUC 0.884, against 0.684 for a deliberately weak surface descriptor of colour histogram,
grid dimensions and density, with precision@1 of 0.65 against a chance rate of 0.035. Ground truth
comes from RE-ARC, whose generators emit unlimited instances of one transformation, so slicing a pool
gives tasks identical in rule and different in every surface respect. Retrieval is therefore not the
obstacle. The obstacle is what retrieval would be for. Initialising a task's adapter from one trained
on \emph{a different instance of the same generator} lowers the final training loss by 30\%, on 7 of
8 tasks, graded by relatedness (same-rule 0.00414, unrelated 0.00569, cold 0.00594) so it is genuine
transfer and not a warm-start artefact --- and it changes truth-in-pool not at all, 6 against 6. Same
rule is the most favourable case any retrieval scheme could return. The adapter fits the
demonstrations better and reaches no new hypothesis, which is what the budget table implies and this
measures directly: training loss was already between 0.0006 and 0.09 on solved and unsolved tasks
alike, so optimisation was never the binding constraint.

\textbf{The augmentation space is not obviously enrichable either.} Three auxiliary objectives, each
sharing a fixed step budget with the demonstration objective so that a winner must beat the real
training it displaced, all changed coverage by nothing: masked-output completion 8 against 8 on
twelve matched tasks, bounding-box crops 3 against 3 on five, rule inversion 4 against 4 on seven.
The two serialization-shrinking variants failed before reaching a GPU, one because the 16-token
vocabulary has no spare token for a run marker, the other because lossless uniform deletion saves
1.0\% of cells while truncating the true answer on 6.7\% of tasks.

\paragraph{One constructive result, and it began as a claim we made without measuring.} Asked whether
a compute-poor entrant could train a base model rather than inherit one, we answered that the blocker
was data rather than GPUs: ARC-AGI-2's difficulty is compositional, its training set is about a
thousand tasks, and procedural generators such as RE-ARC implement ARC-1 rules. Building generators
for ARC-2-style composition, we said, was a larger project than a remaining season. That was an
assertion, and this paper's own discipline says a question about how much structure exists is
answered with a script.

It is answerable in an afternoon, and the answer is no. RE-ARC ships 400 \emph{verifiers}, each a
pure grid-to-grid function implementing one task's rule. Transformations compose, so
$\mathrm{verify}_B(\mathrm{verify}_A(x))$ is a new deterministic rule and 400 verifiers give 159{,}600
ordered pairs. Consistency, the property that normally makes synthetic task generation hard, is free:
both stages are functions, so every instance of the composition obeys the same rule by construction.
What is not free is non-degeneracy, so we require that $B$ runs on $A$'s output, that $B$ visibly
acts, that $A$ remains visible in the result, that the pair is not an identity in disguise, and that
the output is within ARC's dimension and colour bounds.

Exhaustively, \textbf{1{,}440 of the 159{,}600 ordered pairs survive all five filters}, and each
composed rule can be instantiated without limit because the generators supply fresh inputs on demand.
That is more distinct rules than ARC-AGI-2's training set contains, obtained from parts that were
already public, for a few hours of CPU. The honest qualification is diversity rather than count: the
survivors draw on only 44 distinct first stages against 130 second stages, because most verifiers
raise on another verifier's output, each having been written for its own input distribution.
Concentration is mild, the most prolific first stage contributing 7.8\%, so this is 1{,}440 rules and
not a handful in disguise, but the first-stage bottleneck is the obvious place a serious attempt
would start.

We report this as a capability rather than a result, because we have not trained on it and do not
claim it helps. Two things would have to hold and neither is established here: that compositions of
two ARC-1 rules resemble ARC-2's compositional difficulty rather than merely stacking, and that a
corpus consistent by construction is also \emph{learnable}, since a composition is guaranteed
well-defined but not guaranteed to admit a short description. What we can say is that the reason we
gave for not attempting it was wrong, and wrong in a way one script would have caught at any point in
the preceding months.

What survives as advice is therefore narrower and, we think, more useful than a list of ideas. Every
lever we have measured either operates downstream of generation, where the theorem bounds it, or
improves the fit to the demonstrations, where there was no headroom to begin with. The demonstrations
underdetermine the rule and the base model resolves that underdetermination wrongly on 65\% of tasks.
Nothing that reweights, re-searches, re-initialises or re-augments around that fact has moved it
here. We would direct the next entrant at the resolution itself rather than at anything surrounding
it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The 2025 winning recipe survives a single 16~GB consumer GPU, but only after the inference engine is
fixed. That one change is worth more than every other knob, and it is what makes per-task TTT
tractable through an adapter-serving bridge. The honest result is a 12.79\% no-TTT baseline and a
16.28\% regularized-TTT blend on the public evaluation, 13.61\% and then 18.19\% on the hidden leaderboard as the per-task training deepened, with the
gains bounded by adapter overfitting and by a VRAM wall on large grids that later proved to be partly a
context-truncation artifact rather than a true limit. Against that single-card ceiling we set one
measurement from above. A faithful reproduction of a public pipeline that one 16~GB card can hold but not
serve in budget, run on the contest's own four-L4 node and repaired of two defects its community port had
introduced, scores 30.97\%
on the same hidden set. That result is not ours and not single-card, and its value here is that it
prices the constraint at roughly twelve points instead of leaving it an open question.

The lessons that generalize are about measurement rather than about ARC. Public-eval gains must be
re-validated in the deployed condition, since every public-validated candidate lever we submitted
regressed on the hidden set, and the one class that did transfer was repair rather than tuning. A score
displayed beside a public notebook is not a measurement of the code published there, and treating it as
one cost us a week and three quantified explanations of a gap that a single control retired in one
submission. A family of rules is a multiplicity, so a sweep of sixteen selectors is nearly certain to
produce a winner by chance, and in our case the pool's dominance structure refuted the whole family
before any of it ran. And an effect measured against a hard threshold over low-precision arithmetic
needs a same-seed replicate before it means anything, since ours produced an apparent 4.4-point gain
that was entirely the decoder disagreeing with itself. Each of those cost real compute to learn and
none of them is specific to this benchmark.

What we could not move is the part that was never about scheduling or ranking. Across the levers we
closed, the residual is that most tasks never produce the correct grid at all, which is a property of a
4B model rather than of the twelve-hour budget, and the honest next step is a stronger base rather than
another pass over the same candidates. Those bounds belong to one small card, not to
the task. The contest gives four L4 GPUs and 96~GB, where the offline frontier now leads near 66\%, and
the verifier, the TTT bridge, and the selection rule we built are the pieces we would carry onto that
hardware, with the scaling case stated as a falsifiable hypothesis rather than a promise
(Section~\ref{sec:scale}). The 16~GB card is where the climb starts, not where it ends, and the number beneath it keeps moving. The full system is open source, the GGUF conversion recipe for the custom-vocabulary
model, the AIRV pipeline, the per-task TTT bridge and sweep harness, and a development log recording
every measurement and dead end behind these numbers.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

\textbf{Environment.} A single NVIDIA RTX~5070~Ti (16~GB, Blackwell, $\approx 896$~GB/s), native
Windows 11. For inference, a prebuilt llama.cpp CUDA~12.4 \texttt{llama-server}, run on Blackwell through
PTX JIT. For training, PyTorch with a LoRA via PEFT, bf16 base, gradient checkpointing. The two frameworks
are never co-resident in VRAM (Section~\ref{sec:ttt}).

\textbf{The two GGUF converter patches.} The NVARC model has a 16-token \emph{WordLevel} tokenizer,
which trips the stock converter twice. (1)~\emph{Unknown tokenizer hash:} the loader does not
recognize the tokenizer's hash and refuses to guess a pre-tokenizer. Its pre-tokenizer is in fact the
\texttt{qwen2} regex, so we map the hash to \texttt{qwen2} explicitly. (2)~\emph{Empty merge list:} a
WordLevel tokenizer has no byte-pair merges, but the loader requires a non-empty list, so we add two
throwaway merges among the role tokens, which never fire on grid text. Both patches are in the
repository's conversion script and are applied automatically by the Kaggle prep notebook.

\textbf{Serving flags.} \texttt{llama-server} is launched with full GPU offload
(\texttt{-ngl~99}), a 16{,}384-token context, a single slot (\texttt{-np~1}), and the prompt cache
disabled (\texttt{--no-cache-prompt}). Its own logs are redirected to a file so they never flood the
notebook. Decoding is greedy (temperature $0$) with \texttt{n\_predict} sized to the target grid.

\textbf{Hyperparameters.} AIRV uses the eight D4 augmentations and votes to two attempts. Per-task
TTT (regularized): rank 16 to 32, dropout 0.05 to 0.1, 8 to 20 steps, AdamW, a fresh random
augmentation per step, leave-one-out target, context capped at $\approx 1{,}600$ tokens, applied at
adapter scale $1.25$ at inference. Exact values per experiment are in the development log.

representativeness check (no-TTT scores 12.8\% on the subset versus 12.79\% on the full set).

\textbf{Colour permutation.} Adding a random colour permutation to the augmentation set lifts the
no-TTT baseline from 12.8\% to 14.4\% on the 84 tasks, but provides no further gain on top of the
per-task adapter, which has already learned the task's colour mapping, so the two do not stack.

\section{Engineering log of walls and dead ends}
\label{app:log}
We record the failures explicitly, because on this hardware they are most of the work.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{The decoder's batching rests on an undocumented invariant that generated data
breaks.} The DFS entry point builds a single tensor from a batch of augmented prompts, so every view
in a batch must tokenize to exactly the same length, and the batch membership is fixed by hard-coded
index offsets over the sorted view keys. Nothing enforces the invariant and nothing checks it. It
holds on all 120 ARC-AGI-2 public evaluation tasks we measured, because their prompts fit inside the
context window and the sixteen views are then permutations of identical content. It fails on 4 of 24
RE-ARC tasks, whose longer demonstrations trip the length-trimmer into dropping a \emph{different}
example from each view, leaving batch members 506 tokens apart and raising before a single forward
pass. Anyone reusing this decode loop on generated or otherwise longer tasks will hit it. The fix
that preserves semantics is to split a ragged batch by length rather than to pad it, since the
decoder passes explicit positions and padding would silently corrupt them.
\item \textbf{Eager decode is overhead-bound, and the usual fix is unavailable.} Hugging Face eager
decode runs at $\approx 13$ tok/s, about $10\%$ of the bandwidth roofline. \texttt{torch.compile}
would close the gap, but it needs Triton, which has no native-Windows backend. The resolution was to
change engines, not to optimize the eager path.
\item \textbf{The server prompt cache destabilizes unique-prompt workloads.} Enabled by default, it
reserves $\approx 680$~MiB per unique prompt. Every ARC prompt is unique, so it never reuses and
instead destabilizes sustained generation. Disabling it and running a single supervised stream fixed
the stalls.
\item \textbf{4-bit QLoRA is broken on Blackwell + Windows.} bitsandbytes fails to load on this
platform, so the planned 4-bit continued fine-tune was infeasible. Because per-task TTT already runs
in bf16 inside 16~GB, we use a bf16 LoRA throughout and drop the 4-bit path entirely.
\item \textbf{PyTorch decode can turn quadratic after a training step.} Generation in the gradient
framework is not only slow but intermittently degrades after an optimizer step. This is precisely why
the serving bridge exists, since we never generate in PyTorch in the scored pipeline.
\item \textbf{Hugging Face eager stalls offline validation, the same engine lesson again.} The rig
validation scripts that drove generation through eager had no per-task timeout, so one big-grid task could
hang a whole multi-hour run with no way to resume. Moving validation onto the same \texttt{llama-server}
path used for scoring, with a hard per-request cap, a heartbeat file, and per-task checkpoints, removed the
stalls and made the runs resumable. The engine was the bottleneck for validation just as for inference.
\item \textbf{The VRAM wall on large grids was a default, twice over.} The largest-train-pair token
length, not the test grid size, is what grows the training sequence. We first recorded the resulting
exclusion as a hard limit, then found that forcing TTT onto those tasks had only ever been tested under a
six-hundred-token truncation that admits one demonstration, and later that the framework was dispatching
attention to a quadratic kernel because no flash or memory-efficient implementation exists for this GPU in
this build. Forcing the cuDNN backend trains rank 256 at eight thousand tokens in 14.86~GB. The general
lesson is to distrust a wall that was never probed one variable at a time.
\item \textbf{Naive TTT ties the baseline.} The standard adapter configuration drives training loss
to $0.000$ and generalizes no better than no-TTT. Only regularization (fewer steps, lower rank,
dropout) recovers a lift.
\end{itemize}

\section{The mathematics of the measurement}
\label{app:math}

The competition's numbers are statistics over a small finite task set, and several of this work's wrong
turns trace to reading them with more resolution than they possess. This appendix collects the formal
content behind the rules the body applies, what a leaderboard point is, what a paired comparison can
resolve, why a candidate pool cannot rank its own truth, and what the time budget actually optimizes.
Everything here is elementary. The value is that each statement is attached to a measurement from the
body, so the arithmetic and the project's history check each other.

\subsection{The score and its quantum}

Task $i$ has $n_i$ test outputs, the submission supplies two attempts per output, and a task contributes
one credit split evenly across its outputs,
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n_i}\,\Bigl|\bigl\{\, j \le n_i \;:\; y_{ij}\in\{a^{(1)}_{ij},\,
a^{(2)}_{ij}\}\bigr\}\Bigr|,
\qquad \text{score} \;=\; \frac{100\,S}{N}.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
On the hidden set $N=240$, so one credit is $100/240 \approx 0.4167$ percentage points, the quantum the
leaderboard moves in. The public-notebook clusters sit at 31.39, 31.81, 32.22 and 32.64, spaced 0.41 to
0.42 apart, which is that quantum observed directly, whole boards separated by single tasks. The measure
also carries a quiet bias. A solved output on a two-output task earns half what it earns on a single-output
task, so any method that helps preferentially on multi-output tasks is systematically underpaid by the
metric.

\subsection{Resolving power of a paired comparison}

Two variants scored on the same tasks form a paired design, and the pairing removes the between-task
difficulty variance entirely. Concordant tasks, solved by both or missed by both, carry no information
about the difference. Writing $b$ for tasks that flip miss to hit and $c$ for the reverse, the null
hypothesis of no difference makes the flips exchangeable, $b \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(b+c,\tfrac12)$, and the
exact two-sided McNemar test in the cleanest case, every discordant task falling the same way, gives
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; 2\cdot 2^{-(b+c)},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
p \le 0.05 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; b+c \;\ge\; 1-\log_2 0.05 \;\approx\; 5.32.
\label{eq:mcnemar}
\end{equation}
Six unanimous flips are therefore the minimum resolvable effect at the five percent level, $p = 2/64 =
0.031$, while five give $0.0625$ and fail. Every candidate-side lever this work measured moved one to three
tasks. Such effects are not weakly supported, they are unresolvable by any single read of a board this
size. The body's episodes are this bound observed. The regularized-TTT comparison of
Section~\ref{sec:ttt-results}, six flips against one across seven discordant tasks, gives exactly $2 \cdot 8/128 =
0.125$, the $p \approx 0.13$ reported there. The selector switch shipped on three unanimous flips, $p =
0.25$ two-sided, and reversed on the full sample. And the accidental repeat run, two submissions of one
build, differed by 0.41 points, one quantum, which is the empirical noise floor agreeing with the formal
one. Figure~\ref{fig:resolution} places the episodes on the bound.

\centering

\subsection{A family of rules is a multiplicity}

The selection sweep of Section~\ref{sec:selection} scored sixteen rules against one pool. If each rule
independently had probability $\alpha$ of a spurious win, the chance that at least one wins is
$1-(1-\alpha)^{16}$, which at $\alpha = 0.05$ is $1-0.95^{16} \approx 0.56$. The sixteen rules share the
pool, so the tests are positively correlated and the true figure is smaller, but the reading survives the
correction. The sweep's observed yield, exactly one rule gaining exactly one output, is what chance
predicts from a search of that size over a null family, and that is why the winning rule was recorded
rather than deployed.

\subsection{Why the pool cannot rank its own truth}

Model the pipeline as inducing, for each test output, a distribution $q$ over grids, the composition of
the adapted model, its sixteen augmented views, and the depth-first search. The pool is the sampled
support of $q$, and every statistic the pool records for a distinct grid $c$ is an estimate of a
functional of $q$. The multiplicity $m(c)$ counts the augmented decodes that produced $c$. Each decode $g$
carries a beam score $n_{\mathrm{beam}}(g)$, the negative log-probability of its search path, and
augmentation scores $n_{\mathrm{aug},v}(g)$, per-view negative log-likelihoods of $c$ under fresh
augmentations. Both deployed selectors are monotone functions of these statistics,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{K}(c) \;=\; m(c) \;-\; \frac{1}{m(c)}\sum_{g \in G_c}\overline{n}_{\mathrm{aug}}(g),
\qquad
\sigma_{P}(c) \;=\; \sum_{g \in G_c}\bigl(3-n_{\mathrm{beam}}(g)\bigr)
\;+\; \frac{1}{m(c)}\sum_{g \in G_c}\sum_{v}\bigl(3-n_{\mathrm{aug},v}(g)\bigr),
\label{eq:selectors}
\end{equation}
where $G_c$ is the multiset of decodes yielding $c$ and $\overline{n}_{\mathrm{aug}}$ the per-decode mean.
Call $\Sigma_{\mathrm{mono}}$ the class of selectors nondecreasing in $m$ and nonincreasing in every
recorded NLL. Both of Equation~\eqref{eq:selectors} are members, as are all sixteen swept rules.

\textbf{Proposition.} If a wrong grid $g$ Pareto-dominates the true grid $y^\star$ in the recorded
statistics, meaning $m(g) \ge m(y^\star)$ and every NLL statistic of $g$ is at most the corresponding
statistic of $y^\star$, with at least one comparison strict, then every selector in
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{mono}}$ ranks $g$ at or above $y^\star$. \textit{Proof.} Immediate from monotonicity in
each coordinate. \hfill$\square$

The proposition is trivial. Its content is the premise, which is a measurable property of real pools, and
it holds. On every one of the four leaked outputs of Table~\ref{tab:leak} the true grid is strictly
Pareto-dominated inside its own pool, checked on multiplicity, mean augmentation NLL and best beam NLL,
by one wrong candidate on two of the outputs, by two on a third, and by three on the fourth. No monotone
selector can recover any of the four, so the sixteen-rule sweep was not sixteen independent failures, it
was one proposition observed sixteen times. Figure~\ref{fig:dominance} draws the premise.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{fig_dominance}

The dominance counts carry one more consequence, elementary enough to state in two sentences. A selector
in $\Sigma_{\mathrm{mono}}$ that is strictly monotone in some coordinate where a dominator strictly leads
places that dominator strictly above the truth, so a truth with $k$ such dominators ranks at worst
$k+1$, and two dominators already exclude it from any two-attempt selection. Both deployed selectors are
strictly monotone in the coordinates that matter here, and the measured counts, one, one, three and two,
hold simultaneously in the summary statistics and in the exact addends of
Equation~\eqref{eq:selectors}, checked directly on the pool. The consequence splits the leak.
\texttt{3dc255db\_0} and \texttt{4c7dc4dd\_1}, together worth 1.5 credits, are unreachable by the entire
monotone class in either attempt slot, while the two singly-dominated outputs are reachable only at rank
two and only by rules constructed after seeing the answer, which the multiplicity argument of F.3
forbids. So the class ceiling on this pool lies between the attained 9.833 and 10.833 credits, against
the present-in-pool 12.333, and sixty percent of the leak is closed by proof rather than by sweep.

Dominance is what the failure mode predicts rather than an accident. When the model internalizes a wrong
rule, $q$ concentrates on grids consistent with that rule, and the truth enters the pool only as a
low-mass fluctuation, which is precisely low multiplicity and high NLL, that is, dominance. The same
concentration defeats aggregation at cell level. Treating candidates as draws from $q$, if the marginal of
$q$ at a given cell places most of its mass on a wrong colour, a per-cell majority vote converges to that
wrong colour, amplifying the marginal rather than correcting it. The body measured exactly this, thirteen
same-shape candidates fusing to a grid wrong in the same two cells the selected answer gets wrong.

The census of Figure~\ref{fig:pool-census} completes the pool's accounting and adds two measurements
made after the theorem was proved. Counting Pareto dominators over the recorded summary statistics
alone, only one leaked output is blocked for every monotone rule and the bound reads 18 of 60; the
exact-addend analysis above is stricter, excluding a second output and giving 17. Either way two to
three outputs of headroom exist inside the theorem, and the first measurement is that they are not
learnable: refitting the selector weights over multiplicity, augmentation and beam terms with
leave-one-task-out validation attains 14 against the deployed 15, one output \emph{worse}, against a
pre-registered bar of plus two. With four leaked outputs in sixty, weight tuning memorises them, so
the headroom is real but unreachable from data of this size, and the practical ceiling is the deployed
selector's. The second measurement is the shape of what the pool misses. Of the 41 truth-absent
outputs, one sits a single cell from a pool candidate and six more within three cells, but the pool's
errors are correlated through the model's beliefs, weighted cell-wise voting reconstructs none of the
41 under any of three weightings, so the near-misses are not recoverable by aggregation, and the six
outputs where no candidate even has the right shape are beyond every selection-side idea entirely.

An escape therefore needs information not derived from $q$. The round-trip score of
Section~\ref{sec:selection} has the right form, injecting a candidate as a synthetic demonstration and
measuring the mean change in per-token negative log-likelihood of each held-out real demonstration,
\begin{equation}
\Delta(c) \;=\; \frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{i=1}^{|D|}\Bigl[
\mathrm{NLL}\bigl(y_i \,\big|\, D_{-i}\cup\{(x_\star, c)\}\bigr)
\;-\; \mathrm{NLL}\bigl(y_i \,\big|\, D_{-i}\bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:roundtrip}
\end{equation}
with $D$ the demonstration pairs and $x_\star$ the test input. It failed in an instructive direction. The
measured $\Delta$ tracks the description length of $c$ rather than its truth, a simple low-entropy grid
perturbs the conditional barely at all, so $-\Delta$ behaves as a smoothness prior, which is once again a
functional of the model's beliefs. The section's conclusion restated formally, the leak is not a ranking
deficiency inside $\Sigma_{\mathrm{mono}}$, it is $q$ itself pointing at the wrong grid, and a repair must
move $q$, which is training, not selection. Figure~\ref{fig:margins} shows the escape attempt's margins.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{fig_delta_margins}
\caption{Margins of the round-trip signal on the 172-output gate, the true grid's advantage in
$\Delta$ over its best synthetic rival. The truth wins 133 of 172 against a chance rate of one in five,
but the mass hugs zero and the median winning margin of 0.0043 nats sits against a median losing margin
of 0.0031, so the discrimination lives inside its own noise. Outputs beyond 0.06 nats are clipped into
the edge bins.}
\label{fig:margins}
\end{figure}

The negative extends from the monotone class to everything the pipeline can compute about itself. Model
the task as a rule $\theta$ drawn from a prior, with truth $Y^\star = g_\theta(x_\star)$, and let $q$
stand for the trained model's entire belief state on the task, of which the sampling distribution over
grids is the visible part. Let $W$ collect everything the pipeline computes internally, the pool, its
statistics, and any negative log-likelihood the model can be asked to evaluate. Two assumptions. The
pool is exchangeable given $q$, which is how it is generated. And the internal-channel assumption,
$Y^\star \perp W \mid q$, the internal data reads the task only through the model's beliefs. The second
is the load-bearing idealization, and everything below is conditional on it.

\textbf{Theorem (internal ceiling).} Under the two assumptions, every two-attempt rule $\sigma$ that is
a function of $W$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}\{Y^\star \in \sigma(W)\}\bigr] \;\le\;
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\max_{|A|=2}\sum_{c \in A} P\bigl(Y^\star = c \mid q\bigr)\Bigr],
\label{eq:ceiling}
\end{equation}
the expected top-two mass of the posterior given the model's beliefs. \textit{Proof.} Tower over
$(W, q)$, note $\sigma(W)$ is fixed given $W$, replace $P(Y^\star = c \mid W, q)$ by
$P(Y^\star = c \mid q)$ using the independence, and bound the resulting two-term sum by the best
two-term sum pointwise. \hfill$\square$

Equality needs the top-two set of the $q$-posterior to be recoverable from $W$, which holds
approximately once the pool is large. The reading is Blackwell's, the map from $q$ to $W$ is a garbling,
and no processing of a garbled channel creates information about a variable the channel never saw. Two
corollaries follow. The round-trip score of Equation~\eqref{eq:roundtrip} is built entirely from model
likelihoods, so $\Delta$ is a function of $W$ and the theorem bounds it, which retires that family at
theorem level rather than experiment level and explains the measurement, the signal collapsed into a
functional of the model's beliefs because it never had anything else to be. And the escape routes are
exactly the channels that break the assumption, executing a candidate program against the
demonstrations, or scoring under a second independently trained model, both of which read the task
through something other than $q$.

We tested the second escape in the strongest form available offline and it did not pay. Every one of
the 432 candidates in the dumped pool was rescored under the \emph{un-adapted} base, which shares
weights with the deployed model but not beliefs, having never seen the sixteen task-specific
augmentations the adapter trained on. Against the deployed selector's 15 of 60 outputs with the truth
in the top two, that likelihood scores 12 alone, 15 when added to the deployed score, and 14 when
combined with multiplicity: no combination we tried recovers a single additional output, and the
correct-in-pool total it is competing for is 19. The reading is not that a second opinion is worthless
in general but that these particular dominators are not an artifact of the adapter. A belief state that
never saw this task's augmentations ranks them above the truth as well, so the domination is a property
of the candidates rather than of the training, and the cheap reason to expect a second model to help is
gone.

We then ran the version the theorem actually names, rescoring the same pool under the separately
trained 2B sibling of our base, and it appeared to work spectacularly. Its likelihood alone places the
truth in the top two for \textbf{19} of 60 outputs against the deployed selector's 15, and 19 is exactly
the number of outputs whose pool contains a correct grid at all. A perfect score against the reachable
ceiling is not a result, it is a symptom, and the check took five minutes. That model assigns the
correct grid a median per-token loss of \textbf{0.0002}, and it prefers the true grid over every decoded
alternative on 54 of 60 outputs including \textbf{40 of the 41 where the correct grid never entered the
pool at all}. It is not ranking candidates. It has memorized the public evaluation set, and a model that
has memorized an answer scores it highly whether or not it could ever produce it.

A likelihood alone cannot separate memorisation from capability, since a genuinely strong model also
assigns low loss, so we built the audit the claim actually requires and pre-registered the gates that
could kill it. First the encoding was proved exact, the scored token ids decode back to the true grids
on all 172 outputs under both tokenizers with zero mismatches, so the likelihoods are computed on the
truth rather than on an artifact of a sixteen-token tokenizer that had already produced one silent
zero-supervision bug. Then the likelihood was made to predict something falsifiable. A model at 0.0002
nats per token must be able to produce these answers outright, so both models greedy-decoded the public
evaluation set with no test-time training and no search, and the prediction held, the 2B reproduces
\textbf{80\%} of the answers exactly (48 of 60) where the 4B manages 10\%. The decisive arm is fresh
data. RE-ARC ships the generators and verifiers for the ARC-1 concepts, so we minted 120 instances
under our own seed on 1 August 2026, each instance checked by RE-ARC's own verifier, giving a column
that provably postdates every published checkpoint. On that column the ordering reverts to what
capability predicts, the 4B at \textbf{78.3\%} against the 2B's \textbf{65.0\%}. The interaction is
$+83.3$ points with $z = 8.0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:contamination}). A 2B does not beat a 4B from the same
author eight-fold on the harder published set while losing to it on easier fresh data. The narrow claim,
and the only one the evidence licenses, is that the public evaluation set is not a valid measurement
instrument for this checkpoint. Nothing here speaks to intent, training on the public split is standard
practice in a competition scored on a private one; the finding is about what the artifact can measure,
not about the conduct of its author.

The audit reshapes the escape-route ledger rather than closing it. The un-adapted base fails as a
second opinion by agreeing with the dominators, and the 2B's apparent 19-of-19 was recall, so no
\emph{validated} second-model gain exists. But the fresh column also shows the 2B is a genuinely
capable model, 65\% on data it cannot have seen, whose defect is specific to the published split.

We then ran the second route on data neither model could have memorised. Both experts were trained
per task on 24 freshly generated RE-ARC instances, the 4B decoded the pool, and every unique
candidate was rescored under both models over eight fixed augmentations. Of 23 outputs with a
non-empty pool, 18 contained the truth, and the deployed single-expert selector already placed all
18 in its top two. \textbf{The oracle gap available to any fusion of the two experts is 0 of 18.}
There is no headroom on fresh data for a second belief state to recover, because the first one is
not making selection errors there; it is failing to generate. The apparent 19-of-19 on public data
was recall, and the fresh replication shows the route has nothing left to give even when
contamination is removed. We record this as a closure rather than an untested possibility.

The other named escape, executing task-side structure against the demonstrations, was run on the
hidden set as v7.5a: the symbolic arm's object-count signal restored as a pure re-ranking prune,
adding $0.5$ to a beam's score per unit of disagreement with the predicted component count. It
scored \textbf{27.92}, which against eight runs of the unchanged configuration is 2.3 standard
deviations below the mean and therefore \emph{not} a demonstration of harm on its own. The
conclusion below rests instead on the offline invariant-mining experiment, which reaches it with a
within-task comparison that no hidden-set noise touches.
The theorem is not touched, but its escape clause acquires a measured precondition: a task-side
oracle only lifts the bound if it is more accurate than the model's own beliefs about the same
property, and this one, which returns a count on every branch including its no-rule fallback, is
not. A noisy oracle is strictly worse than none, because the bound it escapes is lower than the
penalty it inflicts.

That precondition now has a positive control we did not have to run. Concurrent work evolves Python
transformation programs for ARC-AGI-2 under a fitness that is 90\% \emph{execution} of the candidate
against the task's own demonstration pairs, and reports an open-weights model rising from 12.1\% to
34.0\%, with a stronger model reaching 61.4\%~\cite{imbue2026}. That is the same channel our theorem
names, with the one property ours lacked. Execution either reproduces the demonstrations or it does
not, so the oracle is exact by construction, whereas ours predicted a property from the same
machinery whose beliefs it was meant to correct. Read together, the two results bracket the escape
clause from both sides: exact verification nearly triples an open model's score, and approximate
verification of the same kind of structure costs us three sigma. The route is real, and oracle
accuracy is the whole of it. The open engineering question, which we do not answer, is whether
execution-verified synthesis fits the offline track's roughly \$0.20 per task when the published
demonstration of it runs at \$2.42 to \$8.71.

The contamination finding carries one further caution for anyone building on these artifacts. Any
measurement that uses that checkpoint to score, filter or validate against public ARC-AGI-2 evaluation
tasks will be inflated, and the inflation is invisible unless absolute likelihoods are inspected or
fresh data is minted. We report it because the published model card does not, and because a 19-of-19
result is exactly the kind of number this paper has already been fooled by once. The instrument
generalises beyond this case, establish a capability ordering on data generated after publication,
measure the same models on the suspect set, and read contamination off the interaction; it needs no
access to training data, no private split, and no cooperation from the model's author. It has a
place in a literature. Performance-based contamination testing exists in general
form~\cite{constat}, and regenerating a benchmark from scratch to measure the drop was done for
grade-school arithmetic~\cite{gsm1k}; the field's own 2025 technical report names the ARC version of
the problem, ``knowledge overfitting'', and states explicitly that it does not quantify
it~\cite{arc2025}. The ordering inversion on generator-fresh instances is, to our knowledge, the
first instrument that quantifies it for ARC checkpoints, and the generator that makes the fresh
column possible ships with the benchmark's own ecosystem~\cite{rearc}.

Identifiability closes the pool-only question from below. The pool is exchangeable given $q$, so its
law depends on the draws only through the recorded multiset, the empirical measure is sufficient, and
the best pool-only rule is a function of it. If two rules $\theta_0 \ne \theta_1$ induce the same $q$
but different truths, the pool laws coincide and the posterior odds equal the prior odds, the pool
contributes nothing toward telling the truths apart. A quantitative form needs three hypotheses rather
than two, since with two confusable truths a two-attempt rule submits both and never errs. Take three
rules with pairwise distinct truths whose pool laws sit within total variation $\eta$ of a common
anchor. Any two-attempt rule misses at least one of the three truths pointwise, so the three error
probabilities sum to at least $1 - 2\eta$, and the worst-case error of any pool-only rule is at least
$(1 - 2\eta)/3$, approaching one third as the profiles coincide. The three outputs whose truth was
decoded exactly once are the natural place to hunt for such triples. Published separations sharpen the
margins. With $\Delta_{\mathrm{gap}}$ the gap between the truth's mass and the best wrong mass,
majority-style selection needs order $1/\Delta_{\mathrm{gap}}^2$ samples and best-of-$n$ order
$1/\Delta_{\mathrm{gap}}$, and a nonpositive gap, the measured case here, defeats every sample
size~\cite{huang2025ttscaling}, while coverage-style analyses tie the value of any inference-time
selection to the policy's mass on the truth~\cite{huang2025bon}.

What survives for practice is an estimator design, stated as a proposition. To first order the round
trip decomposes as $\Delta(c) = \alpha K(c) + \beta R(c) + \varepsilon$, a description-length term, a
rule-consistency term, and noise, and the measured pathology is the first term dominating. Let $\pi$
scramble the test input by a fixed cell permutation, preserving the candidate, its size and its palette
while destroying the spatial link between input and candidate.

\textbf{Proposition (control variate).}
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\Delta}(c) \;=\; \Delta(c \mid x_\star) - \Delta(c \mid \pi(x_\star))
\;=\; \beta R(c) + (\varepsilon - \varepsilon'),
\label{eq:cv}
\end{equation}
the complexity term cancels to first order, and detecting a mean effect $\beta R$ at $z$ standard
errors needs $F \ge (z\sigma/\beta R)^2$ held-out folds, with the measured margin scale of 0.0043 nats
setting the bar. The ceiling still applies, $\widetilde{\Delta}$ is built from model likelihoods, so at
best it recovers the consistency component the recorded statistics discard and cannot exceed
Equation~\eqref{eq:ceiling}. Any deployment claim passes through the discipline of F.2 and F.3, stated
before testing, validated on a disjoint pool.

\subsection{Time as the decision variable}

Let $p_i(t)$ be the probability task $i$ is solved when given $t$ seconds, and let $T$ be the wall. The
allocation problem is to maximize $\sum_i p_i(t_i)$ subject to $\sum_i t_i \le T$. For concave increasing
$p_i$ the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions give water-filling, $p_i'(t_i) = \lambda$ on every task receiving
time and $p_i'(0) \le \lambda$ on the rest, marginal solve probability per second equalized. The measured
behaviour of this pipeline is closer to a step. A task completes its search at a natural time $\tau_i$ or
fails regardless, $p_i(t) \approx s_i\,\mathbf{1}[t \ge \tau_i]$, which turns the allocation into a 0/1
knapsack over $(s_i, \tau_i)$. Since $\tau_i$ is unknown until the task is run, the implementable policy
is a uniform cap $C$, and under sequential processing against the wall the outcome is
\begin{equation}
k(C) \;=\; \max\Bigl\{k \;:\; \sum_{i\le k}\min(\tau_i, C)\;\le\; T\Bigr\},
\qquad
S(C) \;=\; \sum_{i \le k(C)} p_i\bigl(\min(\tau_i, C)\bigr).
\label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
Raising $C$ weakly raises every surviving term and weakly lowers $k(C)$, and each task beyond $k(C)$
contributes zero, so $S(C)$ is in general non-monotone and its maximizer can be interior. When the wall
never binds, larger $C$ is weakly better, which is why a 120-task public run cannot exhibit the effect
while a 240-task hidden run can, and why the public and hidden reads of a cap change may legitimately
disagree. The measured inputs are a $\tau$ range of 174 to 1889 seconds on the 40-task slice, a deployed
cap of 1800 on every submission through the wiring fault of Section~\ref{sec:results}. The strongest
public build was believed to run 1200, which is what made the cap the leading candidate for the gap.
An earlier draft withdrew that candidate, on the grounds that the build forks a source hardcoding
1800 and that both sides therefore ran the same cap. That withdrawal read the wrong notebook. It
inspected the build we had forked as a control, which is not the build carrying the score. Pulling
\texttt{mikelou1/arc-agi2-lb33-89-minimal-perfpatch} through the Kaggle API on 1 August 2026 and
reading its source shows a literal 1200-second per-task cap written inside its own decode loop,
on the effective path, inherited from nothing. The candidate was reinstated. The two builds did not
run the same cap, and for two days the cap was again the leading unexplained difference between that
build and ours. This is the second time in this work that a conclusion about a public notebook was
reached without reading the notebook that actually holds the score, and the correction cost nothing
only because the check is a single API call. The control then priced the reinstatement. The
identified build, forked unmodified at its own 1200-second cap, scored 28.47 against our 30.97 at
1800 (Section~\ref{sec:phantom}), so the number the cap was reinstated to explain does not itself
reproduce, and the hypothesis survives only as an untested one-factor question with its empirical
motivation removed.
The model below stands as mathematics and is reported because the cap remains a real lever on a
240-task queue, not because any measured gap is now attributed to it. Whether the wall binds on the
hidden set is not observable from outside,
the model predicts sensitivity to $C$ exactly when $\sum_i \min(\tau_i, C)$ approaches $T$. The
submission built to decide it did not. It moved the cap from 1800 to 1200 and lost 1.53 points, four
credits against a one-credit noise floor, which reads as a violation of the flat-plateau prediction
until one notices that the same run also executed 24 unbudgeted greedy fills through a gate that
treated the string \texttt{"0"} as true. Two factors moved, so the experiment is void rather than
adverse, and the prediction stands untested. We report it as void because a confounded run that
happens to point the way our model does not is exactly the run one is tempted to quietly keep.
One operational corollary came from the same smoke
that validated the cap. Any work placed after the capped loop re-enters as unaccounted growth in
$\tau_i$, the greedy fallback measured about 80 seconds per fill past the cap, so post-loop work must
carry a budget of its own. Figure~\ref{fig:cap} draws the model against the measured timings.

The model has a closed form worth recording, because it turns the cap from a swept knob into a solved
problem given one estimable distribution. Write $F$ for the distribution of the natural time $\tau$,
$\bar F = 1 - F$, $h = f/\bar F$ its hazard, $\mu(C) = \mathbb{E}\min(\tau, C) = \int_0^C \bar F(t)\,dt$
the capped mean, and $\nu(C) = \mathbb{E}[s\,\mathbf{1}\{\tau \le C\}]$ the capped credit. Tasks consume
independent amounts $\min(\tau_i, C)$, so by Wald's identity the queue admits
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[k(C)] \;\approx\; \min\Bigl(n,\; \frac{T_w}{\mu(C)}\Bigr),
\qquad \text{the wall binds} \iff \mu(C) > T_w / n,
\label{eq:binding}
\end{equation}
with $T_w$ the per-worker wall and $n$ its queue share. The numbers are immediate. Net of the deadline
margin and startup, $T_w \approx 42000$ seconds and $n = 60$, so the threshold is about 700 seconds,
and the measured capped mean is $\hat\mu(1800) = 24821/40 = 620.5$ seconds, needing no censoring
correction since the observations are themselves capped at the deployed limit, up to the small
overshoot of the between-batch check. The wall is borderline non-binding, which is the analytic route
to the verdict Figure~\ref{fig:cap} reached by bootstrap, and two independent methods agreeing is the
reason to trust the flat plateau.

In the regime where the wall does bind, the objective is reached-count times credit per reached task,
and the optimum has a first-order condition,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[S(C)] \;\approx\; \frac{T_w}{\mu(C)}\,\nu(C),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[s \mid \tau = C]\;h(C) \;=\; \frac{\nu(C)}{\mu(C)} \;\;\text{at } C^\ast,
\label{eq:foc}
\end{equation}
extend the cap until the hazard-weighted marginal credit per second falls to the average credit per
second. With task values constant the condition reduces to $f(C)\,\mu(C) = F(C)\,\bar F(C)$,
equivalently $C^\ast = \arg\min_C \mu(C)/F(C)$, and that ratio is exactly the fixed-cutoff objective of
restart theory~\cite{luby1993}, whose universal schedule pays a logarithmic factor precisely for not
knowing $F$. Here $F$ is estimable, so the fixed cap is the right target, and the coincidence of the
renewal derivation with the restart objective is a consistency check between two independent routes.
Estimating $F$ beyond the historical cap needs the censoring handled, everything above 1800 is
right-censored, and the product-limit estimator with the capped-mean integral does it, after which a
bootstrap resamples the joint rows to preserve the dependence between training loss, time and yield,
imputes the censored tail, and simulates the four queues. The decision rule ties back to F.2, a cap
change ships only when the bootstrap's lower confidence bound on the gain clears two credits, or a live
paired read clears the six-flip bound.

Three classical problems sit adjacent, and each maps with a break worth naming. Pandora's box gates
inspection on an index, and the deployed loss cut is that gate in disguise, run the expensive search
only when the conditional credit per second given the cheap pre-search signal clears the budget's
shadow price, and the measurement that no task finishing above loss 0.05 ever yielded is the index
saying skip. The stochastic-knapsack adaptivity gap bounds how much any signal-adaptive cap can beat
the best static one, a constant factor, so the static $C^\ast$ is the primary lever and the signal is a
bounded refinement, though the imported constant is heuristic here since the policy classes differ. And
the prophet inequality's half-optimal fixed threshold is the floor under all of it, one fixed cap is
never catastrophically worse than clairvoyance.

\subsection{Composition and coverage}

A heuristic closes the appendix because it frames the benchmark itself. If evaluation tasks are
compositions of $k$ primitives drawn from a library of size $n$, the composition space grows like $n^k$
while any training set covers a fixed $m$ of them, so coverage $m/n^k$ vanishes and memorizing
compositions cannot transfer. The design intent of the benchmark is exactly this regime, and the body
measured its signature, a continued fine-tune on the public training distribution scored 11.63 against
the stock model's 12.79 (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). What transfers is whatever captures the primitives
and the composition operator rather than the compositions, which is the formal reason the interesting
levers in this work kept reducing to training-side changes, and why compression-style approaches that fit
tiny models per task remain interesting despite modest absolute scores.
